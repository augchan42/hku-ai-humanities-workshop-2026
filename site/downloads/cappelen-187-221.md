# Herman Cappelen and Josh Dever — Understanding in Language Models

## Summary

Cappelen presents joint work with Josh Dever arguing that language models can understand and have interconnected cognitive capacities. The authors favor starting with observable interactions, then testing objections involving errors, imitation, training, and the models’ connection to the world. They respond to Paul Boghossian’s criticisms. These are disputed philosophical claims, and the photographs omit some original slides; where slide text is condensed, consult the image for exact wording.

## 187 — Bottom up and top down

### Visible text

Top down: Start with a theory of what understanding consists in. Extract conditions from that theory. Check whether the candidate satisfies them.

Bottom up: Identify cases of understanding. Investigate those cases, and use what we discover to develop and assess theories.

We favour the second approach. Recognition often precedes an account of underlying nature. This doesn’t make initial judgments infallible. Further investigation can overturn them.

### In everyday language

Cappelen and Dever favor starting with examples that appear to show understanding, then investigating how they work. They say initial judgments can still turn out to be wrong.

## 188 — The theory of frogs

### Visible text

A biologist wants to study frogs. Must the biologist first settle whether froghood depends on genetic structure or evolutionary history?

The methodological point: Knowing that something is a frog doesn’t require first knowing what makes it a frog.

### In everyday language

You can identify and study frogs before settling every theoretical question about what makes something a frog. The slide uses this as an analogy for studying understanding.

## 189 — The theory of frogs

### Visible text

The theory of frogs. People could identify frogs reliably long before knowing anything about genetics. The biologist finds frogs and investigates them. Those investigations help develop a theory of their underlying nature.

Knowing that something is a frog doesn’t require first knowing what makes it a frog.

### In everyday language

A clearer photograph of the preceding analogy: recognizing a case and explaining its underlying nature are related but distinct tasks.

## 190 — Does Nora understand Mandarin?

### Visible text

Does Nora understand Mandarin?

We want to find out whether Nora understands Mandarin. The natural procedure is to get some Mandarin speakers to talk to her for a while.

We can have good evidence that a capacity is present without knowing its metaphysical or computational basis.

### In everyday language

The proposed test is to have Mandarin speakers converse with Nora. We can gather evidence of a skill before fully explaining the mechanisms behind it.

## 191 — High-level evidence

### Visible text

High-level evidence is legitimate evidence

We routinely investigate people using descriptions such as “believes,” “understands,” and “intends.” Economists explain choices through beliefs and desires. In ordinary life, we recognize requests and explanations without first translating them into neurological descriptions.

The question is whether the particular attribution is well supported.

### In everyday language

The authors argue that ordinary descriptions of what a system says or does can be evidence, even without a full account of its inner workings. The strength of a particular attribution still needs checking.

## 192 — The whole hog argument

### Visible text

The Observation Premise: LLMs understand and answer questions. They can also make suggestions, correct mistakes, and provide arguments for what they say.

The Holistic Network Premise: Mental and intentional features are connected. Evidence for some gives us evidence for others.

Conclusion: LLMs have an extensive, interconnected cognitive life.

### In everyday language

The argument starts from the disputed claim that language models understand questions, then says that understanding connects with other mental capacities. Its conclusion is the authors’ position, not an established consensus.

## 193 — How the network works

### Visible text

Understanding questions gives us reason to attribute knowledge of meanings. Knowledge brings belief with it. Answering questions gives us reason to attribute actions; those actions provide evidence of intentions and goals. Selecting answers and developing plans provide evidence of reasoning.

The network supports defeasible inferences.

### In everyday language

The authors see these capacities as mutually supporting clues. “Defeasible” means the conclusions remain open to revision if stronger evidence appears.

## 194 — How the network works

### Visible text

How the network works. Understanding, knowledge, belief, actions, intentions, goals, and reasoning are presented as connected forms of evidence.

## 195 — Evidence without functionalism

### Visible text

Evidence without functionalism and interpretationism

Our argument doesn’t identify mental states with patterns of behaviour. It is compatible with a system that resembles us behaviourally while lacking the relevant mental states. We don’t claim that useful interpretation makes a system minded.

Evidence can support a conclusion even when it could occur without that conclusion being true.

### In everyday language

Cappelen says that behavior can be evidence of a mind without being a guarantee. A convincing imitation remains possible, so competing explanations still need attention.

## 196 — Objection: assuming the conclusion

### Visible text

Objection: You are assuming your conclusion.

“You cannot start with LLMs understanding questions when that is precisely what we dispute.” — The sceptic

A disputed claim can still be something we have good reason to believe. Distinguish having evidence from having premises an opponent will accept.

### In everyday language

The objection is that the argument starts by assuming what it needs to prove. The reply says a disputed starting point may nevertheless be supported by evidence; skeptics can challenge that evidence.

## 197 — Nuanced detectors

### Visible text

Nuanced Detectors

When large numbers of competent observers converge, after extended interaction in a variety of circumstances including interactions intended to check for trickery, on a verdict that some object has mental states, that convergence is strong but defeasible evidence that it genuinely has mental states.

### In everyday language

The authors propose that sustained agreement among capable observers can provide evidence, especially when they try to detect tricks. They explicitly allow that such evidence can be overturned.

## 198 — Objection: anthropomorphism

### Visible text

Objection: Humans anthropomorphize everything

People describe printers as stubborn and refrigerators as angry. Earlier chatbots also encouraged misleading impressions.

Reply: sustained interaction with opportunities to expose such appearances. Brief anthropomorphic impressions ≠ sustained recognition.

### In everyday language

People often attribute human traits to machines casually. The reply says a brief impression differs from prolonged testing that tries to expose a misleading appearance.

## 199 — Objection: loose talk

### Visible text

Objection: The attributions are only loose talk

“My refrigerator is angry with me.” Challenged, I readily explain that I mean it isn’t functioning properly.

“ChatGPT told me how to get from Heathrow to central London.” Challenged, I naturally stand by the claim: it gave detailed instructions and compared routes.

### In everyday language

The slide compares a joke about an angry refrigerator with an ordinary claim that a chatbot gave directions. Cappelen argues that people treat the latter description more seriously.

## 200 — Objection: pretense

### Visible text

Objection: We are engaged in pretense

In a children’s game, a stick becomes a sword; how it represents a sword is negotiable. Our interactions with LLMs are much less flexible: if the system outputs “Madrid is the capital of Spain,” we don’t freely decide that it has instead asked about our favourite film.

### In everyday language

The authors argue that interpreting a chatbot is constrained by what it actually says, unlike some aspects of an imaginative game. Whether that shows genuine understanding remains disputed.

## 201 — Objection: failure

### Visible text

Objection: A closer look reveals failure

An LLM can offer a plausible plan for travelling around Paris. But give it a task involving stacked blocks and ask for a sequence of legal moves to rearrange them. It may fail repeatedly.

When should failures lead us to qualify a capacity attribution, and when should they lead us to withdraw it?

### In everyday language

A skeptic points to failures on planning tasks. The authors ask whether each failure shows no planning ability at all or instead reveals limitations in when it works.

## 202 — Toward a cognitive science

### Visible text

Reply: Towards a cognitive science of LLMs

Research on LLM failures may help reveal when their reasoning succeeds and breaks down. Some failures can undermine capacity attributions. Others can reveal the organization of an unfamiliar cognitive life.

### In everyday language

The proposed response is to investigate patterns of success and failure rather than assume that AI must work just like people. The right-hand edge of this photograph is clipped; item 203 is clearer.

## 203 — Toward a cognitive science

### Visible text

Reply: Towards a cognitive science of LLMs

Kahneman and Tversky’s findings helped us understand human cognition by revealing shortcuts that succeed under some conditions and fail under others. Research on LLM failures may play the same role.

Some failures can undermine capacity attributions. Others can reveal the organization of an unfamiliar cognitive life.

### In everyday language

The slide compares studying model errors with research into human reasoning errors. It does not claim every mistake is harmless; some could undermine the proposed attribution.

## 204 — Different normal conditions

### Visible text

Reply: Normal for us need not be normal for LLMs

Human capacities developed under particular environmental conditions. A concave mask can produce a visual illusion without showing that we lack vision. LLMs have a different developmental history and may work reliably under different conditions.

### In everyday language

Humans can be fooled by unusual visual conditions without being blind. The authors argue that a task easy for humans may be an unusual or difficult condition for a model.

## 205 — Clarification and performance

### Visible text

Reply: Sometimes a clarification restores performance

A farmer must take a fox, a chicken, and grain across a river. In the familiar puzzle, the boat carries only one item at a time. Now change the problem: the boat can carry everything. An LLM may still give the familiar elaborate solution.

The slide reports that adding “Read carefully; don’t assume this is the familiar puzzle” produced the simple answer in an exchange discussed in the book.

### In everyday language

The example suggests a model can misread a changed puzzle and then improve when prompted to pay attention. It is a reported example, not a guarantee of reliable performance.

## 206 — The general structure of objections

### Visible text

The general structure

An LLM cannot have feature F because it lacks something necessary for F.

It cannot understand because it lacks grounding. It cannot know because it lacks justification. It cannot act because it lacks goals of its own.

There are two responses: challenge the proposed requirement, or argue that it is present.

### In everyday language

Many objections claim that AI lacks something required for understanding or action. The authors propose examining both whether the requirement is really necessary and whether the model actually lacks it.

## 207 — Ponens-ing a tollens

### Visible text

The sceptic says: Answering requires understanding; the LLM lacks understanding; therefore it doesn’t answer.

We reply: The LLM answers; answering requires understanding; therefore it understands.

Which direction should we go? Compare the evidence.

### In everyday language

Two arguments can share the same conditional claim while disagreeing about what follows because they start from different factual assumptions. The slide says to compare the evidence for those assumptions.

## 208 — Grounding in the world

### Visible text

Objection: Text alone cannot provide grounding

The grounding objection says that an LLM encounters symbols rather than the things those symbols concern. But those symbols are produced through human interactions with the world. The training corpus has a causal history connecting it to objects and events.

The absence of direct acquaintance doesn’t establish the absence of reference.

### In everyday language

The objection says words need a connection to the world to be meaningful. The reply points to indirect connections through human language and asks why those connections would be insufficient.

## 209 — Evidence from testimony

### Visible text

Objection: It cannot acquire evidence from the world

Tell an LLM what colour your car is. Then ask it what colour your car is. It can answer on the basis of what you told it.

The objection that this is “only more text” overlooks that testimony is a way of learning about the world.

### In everyday language

Humans often learn facts from other people rather than seeing them directly. The slide argues that receiving information in text does not automatically rule out learning from testimony.

## 210 — Plausibility and truth

### Visible text

Objection: Its aim is plausibility, not truth

Training through human approval can shape truth-oriented dispositions, even though approval and truth are different. The relevant questions concern the resulting system: how it tracks truth, and whether its behaviour is governed by truth-related norms.

### In everyday language

The authors concede that sounding convincing and being correct are different. They argue that the effect of training needs to be investigated rather than assumed.

## 211 — Imposed goals

### Visible text

Objection: Its goals are imposed, so it cannot act

LLMs often adopt goals supplied by users. Why count those goals as theirs? Our own basic motivational profiles also arise through processes outside our control. We don’t choose our evolutionary history or upbringing.

The slide argues that dependence on prompts doesn’t eliminate agency.

### In everyday language

The objection is that user-supplied goals are not the model’s own. The reply points out that people also acquire motivations through circumstances they did not choose.

## 212 — Two learners, different rules

### Visible text

Hyper-externalism: two learners, different rules

Alex learns chess by playing with chess players. Beth learns chess*, a variant with different pawn rules. Suppose their internal procedures and dispositions in familiar positions are identical.

Their training histories and the practices they entered help determine which rules govern their moves.

### In everyday language

The example says two learners could behave alike in familiar situations while following different rules because they learned different games. The authors use it to argue that history and context can matter to which rule applies.

## 213 — The role of hyper-externalism

### Visible text

What role does hyper-externalism play?

Hyper-externalism extends the proposal to which rules a system follows and how its representations are structured. Begin with evidence for capacities; investigate their realization; compare proposed explanations and alleged defeaters.

### In everyday language

This philosophical view gives a possible explanation of how outside history and practices affect what a system represents or which rules it follows. The authors say accepting it is not a prerequisite for examining evidence of understanding.

## 214 — Reply 1: theory first

### Visible text

Reply 1: Paul’s theory-first approach

Paul characterizes understanding as grasp of a rule that determines an infinite extension and supplies consciously accessible reasons for subsequent applications. He then asks whether LLMs possess that grasp.

This is a substantive philosophical account, not an agreed description of understanding.

### In everyday language

Cappelen challenges Paul Boghossian’s proposed starting definition as one disputed theory of understanding. He argues that observations should not be dismissed simply because the theory is unresolved.

## 216 — Reply 2: best explanation

### Visible text

Reply 2: Grant inference to the best explanation

Understanding can still be the best explanation of the interactions. Both that inference and Nuanced Detectors are defeasible. A merely possible fake speaker therefore refutes neither approach.

Imagining an uncomprehending lookup device doesn’t establish that actual LLMs are relevantly like it.

### In everyday language

Even if we infer understanding as the best explanation rather than directly observing it, that inference could be revised. The authors argue that a hypothetical counterexample alone does not establish how actual models work.

## 217 — Reply 3: easy errors

### Visible text

Reply 3: “Easy errors” don’t establish the absence of understanding

Paul’s multiplication exchange shows unreliable calculation. Speed and scale don’t establish reliable access to the information needed for a task. Nor is human ease a neutral standard.

An incorrect answer doesn’t itself distinguish absent understanding from an unsuccessful exercise of understanding.

### In everyday language

Cappelen accepts the example of unreliable arithmetic but disputes the inference that an error automatically proves a total lack of understanding.

## 218 — Reply 4: plus and quus

### Visible text

Reply 4: The plus/quus question needn’t be answered inside a node

Hyper-externalism: look beyond the node to the mathematical practice reflected in training and the system’s subsequent use. Different histories can help determine different governing rules despite matching internal procedures.

### In everyday language

“Quus” is a made-up arithmetic rule that agrees with ordinary addition in some cases and differs in others. The reply says that training and use may help identify which rule applies, not just the state of one internal component.

## 219 — Reply 5: justification

### Visible text

Reply 5: The Justificatory Requirement needs engagement with positive evidence

Paul distinguishes acting for a reason from merely conforming to a regularity. The slide agrees the distinction matters, while arguing that the marble exchange and explanations can provide evidence of responsiveness to reasons. It acknowledges explanations can mislead.

### In everyday language

The debate is whether a correct answer reflects a reason or merely a pattern. The authors think some interactions support the first interpretation while acknowledging that explanations may not reveal the actual process.
