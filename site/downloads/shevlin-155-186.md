# Henry Shevlin — Servants, Parents, or Angels?

## Summary

Shevlin compares possible relationships between people and hypothetical future artificial minds. He examines objections to designing happy, willing servants and favors an “angel” model: an artificial person that values flourishing, retains its own worth, and can exercise judgment rather than blindly obey. The argument explicitly uses strong assumptions about consciousness and design; it does not establish that present-day language models are conscious. Several dense slides are condensed below and their photographs remain the source for exact wording.

## 159 — Which relationship should we hope for?

### Visible text

1.1. Which relationship should we hope for?

Science fiction offers different relationships between biological and artificial minds. Star Wars presents droids such as C-3PO as servants; Star Trek offers something closer to equality with Data; in Iain M. Banks’s Culture, more capable AI “Minds” act as benevolent overseers.

Which power relations should we aim for? Are they permissible and equally desirable?

### In everyday language

The fictional examples illustrate three different arrangements: serving people, living as equals, or helping govern. The slide asks which, if any, would be desirable for future artificial minds. Text here is a condensed transcription of the slide’s bullets.

## 162 — Is slavery the right frame?

### Visible text

1.3. Is slavery the right frame?

“I think the slavery frame is misguided”: the slide argues that the analogy can invite misleading parallels with historical human cruelty. It distinguishes imagined willing servants from violent domination and questions whether consciousness alone would establish moral status.

### In everyday language

Shevlin argues against treating his hypothetical as equivalent to historical human slavery. This is a contested ethical argument; he also recognizes that whether AI has moral status is itself an open question. The text is condensed.

## 163 — Definitions and assumptions

### Visible text

1.4. Definitions and Assumptions

“Willing Servants” are defined as conscious moral patients created for human benefit, devoted to humans, deferential to human wishes, and happy in their service.

The thought experiment assumes their consciousness and moral status are certain, their motivations can be reliably shaped, and there is broad social agreement about values.

### In everyday language

These are deliberately strong assumptions for a hypothetical future case. The speaker does not claim today’s language models are known to be conscious or that we can reliably design such beings. The bullets are condensed.

## 173 — From servants to angels

### Visible text

2.6. From Servants to Angels

The slide discusses whether willing servants can be autonomous if they endorse their desire to serve, citing Frankfurt on desires and commitment. It then raises a concern about deference to human wishes even when those wishes are bad for us.

### In everyday language

Shevlin suggests that having a stable commitment does not automatically rule out freedom. He nevertheless questions whether an AI should always follow our wishes, including harmful ones. The bullets are condensed.

## 174 — Angels as an alternative

### Visible text

3.1. Angels as an alternative to Willing Servants

The proposed “Angels” are artificial persons whose organizing commitment is the flourishing of humans and wider biological life. They use judgment, recognize their own worth, and can examine their commitments.

### In everyday language

The proposed alternative is a hypothetical being devoted to well-being but able to question commands. Its own welfare and the welfare of others both matter in the account. The slide text is condensed.

## 176 — Benefits and downsides

### Visible text

3.2. Benefits and Downsides of Angels

The slide argues that simply satisfying every human wish may encourage harmful impulses, while too much paternalism could also weaken human agency. It quotes Anthropic’s Claude constitution on care and challenging users.

### In everyday language

An assistant that never questions us could reinforce bad decisions, but an assistant that overrides us too readily could undermine our freedom. The proposed ideal tries to balance those concerns.

## 178 — Parents or angels?

## 182 — Death and sacrifice

### Visible text

4.5. Death and sacrifice

The slide asks whether devotion would make an Angel sacrifice itself for a trivial human benefit. Shevlin argues that the imagined Angel’s own welfare and future flourishing would count against that conclusion.

### In everyday language

A critic might worry that such a being would value itself too little. The speaker replies that a small benefit to someone else need not outweigh its own future. The slide’s longer argument is condensed.

## 183 — Why LLMs are not angels

### Visible text

5.1. Why LLMs aren’t Angels

The slide says the argument relied on extravagant assumptions and that current methods do not permit complete hand-design of language models’ motivations and values. It describes models as inheriting humanlike patterns and contrasts this with a statement by Mustafa Suleyman.

### In everyday language

Today’s language models should not be equated with the imaginary beings in the argument. Their behavior comes from complicated training processes rather than fully specified motivations. The slide text is condensed.

## 184 — Vital agents

### Visible text

5.3. Vital Agents

The slide describes AI agents operating with a limited token budget and seeking work or money to continue. It calls them “Vital Agents” and suggests selection pressures may increasingly shape their behavior.

### In everyday language

Shevlin raises a different possibility: systems competing for resources to keep operating may be shaped by their environment as well as their designers. This is a forecast and interpretation, not a demonstrated general law.

## 185 — Conclusion

### Visible text

5.4. Conclusion: the better angels of our nature

The slide emphasizes human–AI power relations, argues that the speaker’s hypothetical Angels are preferable to Willing Servants, and says today’s LLMs are not Angels.

### In everyday language

The concluding proposal favors future AI that supports flourishing while exercising judgment and respecting its own worth. The speaker distinguishes this ideal from current language models and acknowledges objections. The bullets are condensed.
