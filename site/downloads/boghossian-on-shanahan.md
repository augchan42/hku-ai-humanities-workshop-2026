Comments on Shanahan
Paul Boghossian, NYU

1. Can an LLM feel pain?
2. What exactly are you asking?
3. I can say: When I have pain, I have this characteristic feeling, this sensation, which is individuated by
how it feels, by its phenomenology.
a. And I want to know whether an LLM has that.
4. People have tried any number of phrases to focus attention on this phenomenal property:

a. there is something that it’s like to be in pain; or sensations are unique in that one cannot be in

them without being aware that one is in them; and so on.
5. Descartes started the trend of saying that, if you think about it, these sensations are ‘private’ in a very
strong sense:
i. To you your own sensations are self-intimating in a way that they are not to others.
ii. You are infallible about them in a way that others couldn’t be.
tii. You have a directness of access to them that others could not have.

b. This grounds a thesis of strong, modalized, asymmetry between your access to your own
sensations and that of others.

c. These features have been disputed.

d. One thing is clear: these sensations are real. We can debate whether they have these strong
privacy features.

e. Another thing is clear. Even if they don’t have these strong modal privacy features, at the
moment, they are at least private in a contingent sense: perhaps in principle someone can
gain as good access to my sensations as I have, but at the moment I have strongly
asymmetric privileged access.

6. Given this perhaps merely contingent privacy, it’s very natural to think that our sensation terms refer
to these phenomenologically individuated events: ‘pain’ refers to the sensation of being in pain.

a. Given that fact, our question about LLMs seems like a perfectly factual question: do they
have these feelings, do they have states with this kind of phenomenology?

b. Not only does it seem factual; it doesn’t even seem vague in the way most questions can be:
there is either the sensation or there isn’t.

c. It may be very difficult to answer this question in a definitive way; it may even be impossible
to do so.

d. But the question seems like a perfectly factual one.

7. Murray wants to say, following Witt, that our question is not a factual question.

a. There is no fact of the matter about whether LLMs feel pain.

b. It calls for a decision on our part whether to extend sensation talk to these entities, something
that will reflect an ‘existential orientation.’

8. This thesis could be meant in one of two ways:

a. It could be a view just about mental terms, and some other specific categories of concept.
The view would be that mental terms have ‘open texture.’

i. That is, their meaning does not determine a fully determinate yes/no verdict as to
whether something is or is not in their extension.

b. Or it could be part of a very general view of language according to which all terms are like
that.

c. I couldn’t always tell which of these views Murray had.

d. Let’s suppose it’s the weaker and more defensible view.

9. Why does he hold that pain talk has an ‘open texture’ when it comes to AI systems?

a. Doesn’t the story I told about how I attach ‘pain’ to a specific type of sensation fix a

determinate extension?

10. The Wittgensteinian answer is, no: We know from the ‘private language argument’ that that story
about how sensation terms get their meaning is false.

a. I have never seen a version of the PLA that I thought was any good, so I am not moved by
that. (That includes Kripke’s sophisticated version.)
b. A version that M seems to like goes like this.

i. Suppose you have a sensation type that you haven’t had before; you focus your
attention on the distinctive way in which it feels, and you call sensations of that type
S.

ii. A week later you have what seems to you to be the same sensation type occurring
again, so you think: Ah, S again.

iii. Witt says: What could be your criterion for whether it was S again or not? Your
sensation is private, so no one else can say. You would be the only authority, so
whatever you say would have to be deemed correct.

iv. However, if whatever you say goes, then that only means that we can’t here talk
about correct and incorrect.

v. So that means no determinate extension was fixed via this private procedure.

vi. And that’s why we need to look elsewhere for how the meaning of pain talk is fixed.

vii. And here we get a quasi-behaviorist story, acc to which although pain talk isn’t a
name for some kind of behavior, still it is only at home in a setting in which there
are embodied creatures who behave roughly as we do.

viii. So that’s why, if we are to extend our talk to disembodied creatures or differently
bodied creatures, a decision is called for.

11. I don’t really understand the alternative picture of pain language. But I also don’t think the case has
been made that we need it. Nothing has been shown to be wrong with the private procedure. The
flaw in this argument is the transition from iii to iv.

a. iii is an epistemological claim – by what means could you tell that it is the same sensation type
again.

b. Well, what could I use if not my memory? But suppose my memory is faulty, then there will
be, at the moment, no way to correct that. No one else knows what sensation I am naming.
So, if I make a mistake, we may be stuck with that forever.

c. But what the argument needs is a metaphysical claim: there cannot be a fact of the matter
about whether this was S again or not.

d. The transition from iii to iv says that if the epistemological point is true, then the
metaphysical point is true.

e. But that doesn’t follow: it doesn’t follow from the fact that some fact is not independently
verifiable that there was never a fact of the matter about it to begin with.

i. To suppose otherwise is to endorse a crude verificationism according to which there
can only be facts if they can be verified.

ii. No one should hold such a view: it’s overwhelmingly plausible that Goldbach’s
conjecture is either true or false, but it may also be the case that it will never be
proven or disproven.

12. But the only thing that matters for the question about LLMs to be factual is whether there is a fact of
the matter; not whether it can be verified.

a. And there will be such a fact of the matter if, in the initial baptism, I did zero in on a specific
sensation type and named it.
b. Nothing I have seen has shown me that that can’t be done.

13. That’s why I reject the PLA and why I can’t go along with M’s claim that our question is not a factual

one.
