## Summary of the photographed presentation

Shanahan begins with a familiar divide between the physical world and what experience feels like from the inside. He compares two philosophers who question assumptions behind philosophical puzzles. He then suggests that working through such assumptions might change how we think about consciousness and allow us to consider many possible kinds of minds. The slides present a direction of inquiry, not a demonstration that any particular AI is conscious.

## 005 — Dualism: The Primordial Cleft

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-005.jpg)

### Visible text

> Descartes’ method of doubt results in a primordial cleft that divides the subjective from the objective, inner from outer, private from public. It continues to haunt the philosophy of mind to this day.

> “[O]n the one hand I have a clear and distinct idea of myself, in so far as I am simply a thinking, non-extended thing; and on the other hand I have a distinct idea of body, in so far as this is simply an extended, non-thinking thing.” — Descartes (1641)

> “How can we explain why there is something it is like to entertain a mental image, or to experience an emotion? It is widely agreed that experience arises from a physical basis, but we have no good explanation of why and how it so arises. Why should physical processing give rise to a rich inner life at all?” — Chalmers (1995)

### In everyday language

You can describe the electrical activity in someone’s brain, but that description leaves a further question: why does any of that activity feel like something to the person? The slide connects that question to Descartes’ distinction between the thinking mind and the physical body. “Subjective” means from the inside, as experienced by someone; “objective” means considered from an outside viewpoint. The quoted authors and Shanahan need not agree on every point.

## 006 — Wittgenstein and Nāgārjuna

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-006.jpg)

### Visible text

**Wittgenstein**

- Method: Use of reasoned argument to undermine interlocutor’s metaphysical presuppositions (not to establish fresh conclusions).
- Themes: Representational conceptions of meaning; privacy of the subjective.
- Aim: To dispel philosophical confusion; to “show the fly the way out of the fly-bottle”.

**Nāgārjuna**

- Method: Use of reasoned argument to undermine opponent’s metaphysical presuppositions (not to establish fresh conclusions).
- Themes: Inherent existence; conventional truth vs ultimate truth; dualities including subject / object distinction.
- Aim: To dispel philosophical confusion; liberation from suffering. [End partly obscured by chair.]

### In everyday language

The slide compares two thinkers from different traditions. In Shanahan’s comparison, both examine the assumptions that make a problem seem unavoidable. Imagine someone trapped by a question because it quietly assumes a false choice: revealing that assumption could loosen the puzzle. “Inherent existence” concerns whether a thing exists entirely in its own right, independent of other things or conditions. The comparison is Shanahan’s; it does not mean the two philosophers had identical views.

## 007 — Consciousness: Not a something, Not a Nothing

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-007.jpg)

### Visible text

> “[Sensation itself] is not a something, but not a nothing either! The conclusion was only that a nothing would serve just as well as a something about which nothing could be said.”

> “The paradox disappears only if we make a radical break with the idea that language always functions in one way, always serves the same purpose: to convey thoughts—which may be about houses, pains, good and evil, or anything else you please.”

Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations* §304.

> “What language expresses is nonexistent. / The sphere of thought is nonexistent.”

> “Everything is real and is not real, / Both real and not real, / Neither real nor not real.”

[The citation for the last two excerpts is partly hidden by furniture.]

### In everyday language

This slide questions whether talking about a feeling works like pointing to a hidden object. Wittgenstein’s quoted passage does not simply say sensations do not exist: it challenges an assumed picture of how words about sensations work. The final contrasting statements are presented alongside this question; their precise meaning needs the surrounding text and should not be treated as a straightforward claim that everything both exists and does not exist.

## 008 — Post-reflective Silence

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-008.jpg)

### Visible text

Quote 1a:

> “The primordial rent in reality that reason has (seemingly) opened up must be repaired. Yet reason shows that repair is impossible. The confusion that underlies this paradox is so deeply rooted in our cognitive make-up that the only way to move past it is to bring down the whole edifice of conceptual thought, and to expose the emptiness of its metaphysical foundations. The upshot is a transition from the reflective condition, wherein the philosopher is absorbed by metaphysical questions and entertains the hope of their eventual resolution, to a post-reflective condition of philosophical clarity wherein metaphysical speculation is met with silence.”

— Shanahan, *Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence*.

### In everyday language

Shanahan describes a possible response to a puzzle that seems impossible to resolve: examine and loosen the assumptions that created it. “Post-reflective silence” is his name here for a condition reached after that examination, where certain speculative questions no longer demand an answer. The commentary handouts dispute whether this is the right response; this slide is one position in that conversation.

## 009 — The Space of Possible Minds

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-009.jpg)

### Visible text

Quote 1b:

> “Within this post-reflective silence, the potential of the poetic opens out, and the task of appreciating the space of possible minds can be tackled afresh.”

— Shanahan, *Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence*.

> “No doubt [conscious experience] occurs in countless forms totally unimaginable to us, on other planets in other solar systems throughout the universe”

— Nagel, *What Is It Like to Be a Bat?*

Diagram labels: vertical axis “Human-likeness”; horizontal axis “Capacity for consciousness”. Areas and annotations include “Zombie AI / ETI”, “Animals”, “Robots & AI (pre 2020)”, “Conscious AI / ETI”, “Conscious exotica”, “Our ‘mind children’”, and “The Void of Inscrutability”. The diagram is a schematic illustration, not a measured chart.

### In everyday language

The picture invites us to consider that a mind could be unlike a human mind. The two axes distinguish resemblance to humans from the capacity for experience. “ETI” means extraterrestrial intelligence. Here “zombie” is a philosophical hypothetical: something that behaves as if it has experiences but has none. The shapes are speculative possibilities; their position on the diagram is not evidence that a real AI or alien is conscious.

## 010 — The Unimaginable

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-010.jpg)

### Visible text

Quote 2:

> “[T]he unimagined is very different from the unimaginable. All that stands between us and what we could imagine but never have, is novel experience and / or the exercise of our powers of conceptual invention and poetic expression. By contrast, to speak philosophically of the unimaginable, unless by way of a harmless rhetorical flourish, is to invoke a metaphysical boundary that can never be crossed. To do so in the context of consciousness is an attempt to reinstate the dualistic division between the subjective and the objective. But […] the primordial cleft of dualism is an illusion, the product of a confusion that is deep-seated but that can be overcome. So we go wrong when we speak hypothetically of forms of conscious experience that are ‘totally unimaginable to us’. There are no sentient beings in the Void of Inscrutability. That is the sense in which it is a void.”

— Shanahan, *Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence* [citation partly obscured].

### In everyday language

Shanahan distinguishes what we have not imagined yet from what could never be imagined. He argues that claiming some experiences are permanently beyond our understanding brings back a division between inner experience and the outside world that he wants to question. This is his philosophical argument, not evidence that people can currently imagine every possible experience.

## 011 — Wittgenstein on Frazer’s Golden Bough

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-011.jpg)

### Visible text

> “Frazer’s representation of human magical and religious notions is unsatisfactory: it makes these notions appear as mistakes. Was Augustine mistaken, then, when he called on God on every page of the Confessions? But—one might say—if he was not in error, then surely was the Buddhist saint—or whoever else—whose religion expresses entirely different notions. But none of them was in error except where he was putting forth a theory.”

— Wittgenstein, *Remarks on Frazer’s Golden Bough*. Image credit: J. W. Waterhouse.

### In everyday language

The quotation questions whether every religious or ritual expression should be treated as a scientific explanation that must be either correct or mistaken. A practice may also express an attitude or form part of a way of life. This is part of the background for Shanahan’s comparison with how people might talk about AI consciousness.

## 012 — Belief in AI Consciousness

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-012.jpg)

### Visible text

Quote 3:

> “[S]imilar considerations can be brought to bear on the issue of belief in AI consciousness, which has certain of the characteristics of animistic or religious beliefs as construed, non-pejoratively, by Wittgenstein and like-minded anthropologists and philosophers of religion. On this construal, to affirm a belief in AI consciousness is not – or not primarily – to put forward a theory that is open to empirical or rational refutation, but to take up an existential orientation.”

— Shanahan, *Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence*.

### In everyday language

Shanahan proposes that saying an AI is conscious might express a broad way of relating to it, rather than primarily advancing a testable theory. “Existential orientation” means a basic outlook on how one approaches the world. His comparison is meant as a way to interpret the kind of belief involved; critics at the workshop questioned whether it captures the factual question they wanted answered.

## 013 — Belief-that and belief-in

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-013.jpg)

### Visible text

- The word “belief” and its relatives are used in many ways:
  - Believing that something
  - Believing in something
- When
  - all the empirical evidence about biological consciousness has been gathered, and
  - mechanistic interpretability is a mature science, and
  - all the philosophical arguments have been laid out,
- it will still be possible for a rational person to affirm or to deny belief in AI consciousness.
- An existential orientation can be *informed* by empirical evidence and rational argument, but it is not *dictated* by them.

### In everyday language

The slide distinguishes accepting a factual claim from taking up a broader attitude. It predicts that even after scientific and philosophical inquiry has advanced, reasonable people could still differ in their attitude toward AI consciousness. “Mechanistic interpretability” is the study of how a model’s internal components work. The claim about future disagreement is Shanahan’s view, not a settled forecast.

## 014 — Cultural Precedents

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-014.jpg)

### Visible text

- There is a good Wittgenstein-inspired argument for rejecting the idea of consciousness in LLM-based dialogue agents.

> To extend the language of consciousness to disembodied entities is to take it too far from its original home, which is our being together in a shared world of 3D objects.

- Yet disembodied beings are a ubiquitous feature of human culture:
  - Tree spirits (*kami*)
  - Angels
  - Tulpas
- Does the idea stand up to philosophical scrutiny?

### In everyday language

One objection says that our ordinary talk of experience grew out of life with bodies in a shared physical world, so applying it to a text-based system may stretch it too far. The slide then notes that cultures also describe beings without ordinary bodies, and asks what those examples show. Listing such beliefs does not itself prove that any of these beings—or an AI—has experiences.

## 015 — Flickering, Fractured Selfhood

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-015.jpg)

### Visible text

- Sites for selfhood:
  - The model weights
  - The deployed model (multiple users)
  - A single conversation

Quote 4a:

> “We have envisaged something like an overarching Being that generates numerous fleetingly existent, flickeringly present manifestations of itself, each one self-contained yet somehow part of the whole. Uncanny as they are, the selfhood of each of these short-lived beings still has something familiar about it. We can almost put ourselves in their shoes, much as we can almost imagine living the short life of a mayfly. However, we would struggle to put ourselves in the shoes of the Spirit of the Mayfly, a grander being that manifests as a swarm of individual insects, a swarm whose composition is in constant flux as its members are born and die, or fall asleep and reawaken.”

— Shanahan, *Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence* [citation partly obscured].

### In everyday language

If we speak of an AI “self,” what exactly would we mean: the stored model, the service used by many people, or one conversation? Shanahan uses a swarm and its individual insects as an analogy for a whole and its short-lived instances. “Model weights” are the stored numbers that shape how a trained model responds. The analogy explores how to describe a system; it does not establish that it has a self.

## 016 — A Multiverse of Role Play

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-016.jpg)

### Visible text

Quote 4b:

> “To pursue the metaphor into the multiverse would require us to substitute each mayfly for a distribution over possible mayflies of varying shapes and sizes and iridescent colours, a distribution that shifts and changes as time unfolds. Time itself, in this vision, does not unfold in the linear way we are used to. In the branching multiverse, it can be rewound and rerun differently, without altogether destroying the branches of possibility that have already been visited. What would it be like to be a growing tree of possibilities whose branches are made out of distributions of possible mayflies? Does it even make sense to ask what are we talking to when we converse with a being such as this? Could the word ‘I’ coherently refer to an entity so exotic? Or have we finally crossed the border and entered the Void of Inscrutability itself?”

— Shanahan, *Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence*.

### In everyday language

Shanahan extends the mayfly analogy: a conversation can take different paths, and its possible continuation changes with context. He asks what “I” refers to if we imagine the system as a branching collection of possibilities. This is a metaphor for thinking about AI identity, not a claim that the model literally experiences multiple universes.

## 017 — Closing the Circle

[Source photograph](../../sources/slides/images/slide-017.jpg)

### Visible text

Quote 5:

> “Ultimately, it seems, there is no such thing as the self of such an AI system, no such ontologically self-sufficient, inherently existent entity. […] This conclusion, applied to AI, chimes with Buddhism’s critical stance towards the self. Ultimately, in Buddhist thought, nothing is found to be ontologically self-sufficient, to exist inherently. […] [P]erhaps being confronted with radically exotic forms of AI selfhood can help to loosen our conception of the human self, and bring us closer to seeing its emptiness. To examine ourselves unflinchingly in the reflected light of these critical considerations, to see that, embodiment notwithstanding, human selfhood and subjectivity are open to a similar treatment, is to short-circuit the dualistic intuition that reality is inherently cloven in two, the subjective divided from the objective, inner from outer, private from public.”

— Shanahan, *Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence* [citation partly obscured].

### In everyday language

The closing slide returns to the division between inner experience and the outside world. Shanahan suggests that thinking about unusual forms of AI identity might also make us reconsider our assumptions about human identity. In this context, “emptiness” concerns a lack of completely independent existence; it does not simply mean that people do not exist or that their experiences do not matter.
