## Summary of the photographed set

Cappelen asks what Shanahan’s proposed philosophical “therapy” is meant to accomplish, whose assumptions need treatment, and why clearing up a confusion should end inquiry. Using memory, Descartes, and a whirlpool as examples, he argues that several conclusions need further support. He also points to philosophers who challenge familiar assumptions while continuing to argue and investigate. His comments are objections to Shanahan’s manuscript, not a neutral summary of that manuscript.

## 020 — Different methods, different jobs

### Visible text

- Conceptual analysis: investigating a concept’s content or structure.
- Conceptual engineering: assessing and improving our conceptual resources.
- Formal regimentation: clarifying claims and their consequences through precise formulations.
- Explanatory theorising: comparing accounts by their explanatory merits, often using empirical evidence.
- The method of cases: testing general claims against judgements about particular cases.
- Model-building: using simplified representations to investigate a phenomenon.
- Transcendental arguments: identifying necessary conditions for accepted features of experience or thought.
- Genealogical inquiry: investigating the origins of concepts or practices and their significance for justification.

These approaches overlap. None automatically commits its practitioners to self-sufficient objects or an intrinsically private subject.

### In everyday language

Philosophers use several tools: clarify a concept, improve it, test it against examples, construct a simplified model, or ask what must be true for something familiar to be possible. Cappelen says using such tools does not by itself mean accepting a particular picture of a separate, private inner self.

## 021 — Why should criticism end in silence?

### Visible text

Pyrrhonian/Fogelin Reading of Wittgenstein and Buddhism: Why should criticism end in silence?

Pyrrhonism: When competing considerations appear equally persuasive, or concepts are deconstructed, judgement is suspended. Result: Tranquility of some kind.

Arguments expose difficulties in our commitments or concepts; confrontation with those dissolves the demand for metaphysical answers.

Why should the result be silence rather than revision? Finding a contradiction gives us a reason to reject at least one commitment. It does not establish that the inquiry should be relinquished.

Which methods remain available after therapy? Which must go, and why?

### In everyday language

One response to conflicting arguments is to suspend judgment. Cappelen asks why finding a problem in our assumptions should mean stopping the investigation. Perhaps we can revise an assumption and keep asking questions. “Metaphysical” questions here concern broad claims about what exists and what it is like.

## 022 — Radical criticism without post-reflective silence

### Visible text

**Williamson:** Williamson’s argument against luminosity challenges privileged access to our mental states. It specifies assumptions about knowledge and gradual change that opponents can dispute.

**Cappelen & Dever:** In *The Inessential Indexical*, Josh Dever and I challenge arguments for the distinctive significance of first-person thought. We examine whether their examples establish anything distinctive about the first person.

**Frankish:** Frankish’s illusionism challenges familiar conceptions of phenomenal consciousness and undertakes to explain their appeal.

These projects attack entrenched pictures while continuing philosophical inquiry.

What do they leave undone that the book’s therapy accomplishes?

### In everyday language

Cappelen lists three research projects that question familiar ideas about our minds. His point is that philosophers can challenge deeply held assumptions while still making arguments and seeking explanations. He asks what additional work Shanahan’s approach performs.

## 023 — Who is the patient?

### Visible text

The manuscript finds the spectre of dualism in:

“almost every philosopher or scientist” writing about consciousness. — p. 12

It questions the usual way of thinking in Western philosophy:

“especially in the analytic tradition.” — p. 19

These are claims about an enormous body of work.

Quotations about knowing that we are conscious do not establish commitment to an ontologically self-sufficient, infallibly accessible inner realm.

Where does that commitment enter the arguments?

### In everyday language

Cappelen asks which specific thinkers hold the assumptions the manuscript criticizes. Knowing that you are conscious does not necessarily mean you believe the mind is a separate, independent realm that you can never be wrong about. He wants those assumptions identified in actual arguments.

## 024 — Am I the patient? Is Williamson? Hawthorne? Thomasson?

### In everyday language

Cappelen applies his question to himself and other philosophers. If the book identifies a mistaken assumption, he asks where particular writers actually make it. The “patient” is a person or position supposedly affected by the confusion, not a medical patient.

## 025 — Anti-Exceptionalisms About Philosophy

### Visible text

The book preserves ordinary life and scientific inquiry: “It’s only when we step into philosophy that we go wrong.” — p. 21 [quotation and page number read with some uncertainty because the image is blurred].

Williamson: anti-exceptionalism about philosophy — it just develops methods already used in ordinary life and science. Becoming more general or abstract does not introduce a new metaphysical commitment.

Compare:

- Did this patient consent?
- What makes consent valid?

Same with knowledge, the self, vagueness, counterfactuals, causation, modality, …

Why would discussing these commit us to “ultimate reality” or self-sufficient entities or anything beyond the ordinary?

### In everyday language

The slide considers the idea that philosophy uses and extends ordinary ways of reasoning. Asking whether a person consented and asking what makes consent valid are connected questions, even though the second is more abstract. Cappelen asks why becoming more abstract must introduce a mysterious view of reality.

## 026 — Proust and Memory

### Visible text

On page 17, the argument moves from the absence of external criteria for checking whether a recovered experience is the same to: “the very concept of identity here is meaningless.”

Big jump: The absence of an external check does not entail meaninglessness. The passage supplies no argument connecting them.

And then there’s a further conclusion: *that the original experience is not a thing*. That also doesn’t follow.

Again, I think clarity about the method would be helpful: Does the method license this kind of argument for some reason?

### In everyday language

Suppose a memory brings back a feeling, but nobody has an independent way to check whether it exactly matches an earlier feeling. Cappelen argues that difficulty checking does not by itself show that the question has no meaning. This parallels an objection in Boghossian’s handout, though the two commentaries are separate contributions.

## 027 — Descartes on Self

### Visible text

On page 24: *Descartes’s thought reaches us through language.*

Concludes with the claim that the subject becomes acquainted with itself: “by taking a detour through words.”

But: distinguish a) our access to Descartes’s thought and b) Descartes’s access to himself.

Descartes is dead, who is the patient? Most of us reject the Cartesian picture. Does the invalidity of the argument matter or is that okay when doing therapy?

### In everyday language

We learn what Descartes thought through his words. Cappelen says that is a different question from how Descartes understood himself. He also asks whether an argument must be logically sound if its goal is to change the reader’s outlook. “Cartesian” refers to ideas associated with Descartes.

## 028 — The whirlpool: what follows?

### Visible text

On pages 26–27, the book argues that a whirlpool lacks precise boundaries and depends on conditions outside itself.

Its individuation is treated as conventional. The book then says: “The point generalises to all phenomena.”

1. Why does difficulty specifying a boundary establish dependence on our conventions?
2. Why does the conclusion about a whirlpool generalise to everything?

A reader could accept that whirlpools are derivative while holding that other things are fundamental.

The example alone does not establish either transition: is it still therapeutic?

### In everyday language

A whirlpool depends on moving water, and its precise edge can be hard to draw. Cappelen asks whether those observations show that the whirlpool depends on our way of describing it, and whether the same conclusion applies to everything. Those are additional steps that he thinks need arguments.

## 029 — Vagueness: the argument bypasses its opponents

### Visible text

**Abundant ontology:** An abundant ontology allows many overlapping objects with different precise boundaries. Our expression might fail to select one determinately. That would leave their existence untouched.

**Epistemicism:** An epistemicist can hold that there is a determinate boundary, although we cannot know it. Hawthorne and McGonigal combine epistemicism with a plenitudinous metaphysics.

These massively discussed positions address the difficulty the example raises so would be helpful to, say, a student, if the argument engaged them. But are students patients?

### In everyday language

The slide offers two possible responses to a fuzzy boundary. One says there may be many overlapping candidate objects, while our words do not pick one precisely. Another says there is an exact boundary but we cannot know where it is. Cappelen wants the book to engage with existing alternatives before drawing its conclusion.

## 030 — “Self-sufficient”: which independence?

### Visible text

On page 18, ultimate reality concerns whether something’s existence: “transcends our language games and the framework of concepts they imply.”

On page 27, the whirlpool depends on: “causes and conditions external to itself.”

Dependence on causes does not establish dependence on language.

A realist can accept that a whirlpool depends on water and gravity while denying that it depends on our descriptions.

Is this still good therapy or does that require good arguments?

### In everyday language

A whirlpool needs water and gravity in order to exist. Cappelen says that does not automatically mean it needs humans to name or describe it. He distinguishes depending on physical causes from depending on our language.

## 031 — Taking stock

### Visible text

**Who is the patient?** Which contemporary thinkers hold the commitments being treated? Where do those commitments enter their arguments? A diagnosis of “Western philosophy” is too broad to answer these questions.

**What would count as successful therapy?** Is success the reader’s loss of interest in metaphysical questions? Or must the reader have good reasons to abandon them? What would show that the diagnosis was mistaken?

**Is the therapy compatible with anti-exceptionalism about philosophy?** If philosophical inquiry develops methods already used in ordinary life and science, why should removing confusion require philosophical silence? Why couldn’t the inquiry continue with corrected assumptions?

**Can defective arguments provide legitimate therapy?** An invalid argument might change someone’s outlook. What makes that change a philosophical achievement? Calling an argument a ladder to be discarded does not explain why its missing steps cease to matter.

### In everyday language

Cappelen ends with four questions: whom is the book addressing, how do we tell whether its approach worked, why stop inquiry after fixing a confusion, and do weak arguments still matter if they change someone’s perspective? These questions summarize his critical response rather than settling the dispute.
