Comments on Shanahan - Nate Sharadin : 5 November 2026 : bare page numbers are the manuscript’s

• 1. Price’s M-worlds:

— “the four Ms, for example: Morality, Modality, Meaning and the Mental”: “important areas of human discourse
seem threatened by the rise of modern science” (Price, “Naturalism and the Fate of the M-Worlds”, 1997).

— Price 2013, p. 5: “in many interesting cases it is hard to see what natural facts we could be talking about.
Different people will offer different lists of these ‘hard problems’ — common candidates include meaning, value,
mathematical truth, causation and physical modality, and various aspects of mentality”.

— Consciousness talk lives in one of them: the Mental.

— Morality: the first M, and Price’s own test case: “Some topic of human discourse—morality, say-seems difficult
to accommodate in a naturalistic world view.” (Price 1997, §1).

• 2. Why go pragmatist? Because the M-worlds seem suspicious!

— Eliminate them and we lose too much; defend them as non-natural and we need a faith in the limits of science.
So “most philosophical naturalists look for some sort of rescue strategy” (Price 1997).

— The pragmatist route “offers the benefits of nonnaturalism, without the metaphysical down side” (Price 1997);
“no metaphysical spooks are thereby required.” (Price 1997, §2)

— Morality: the same menu, with names attached. Eliminate: Mackie, “There are no objective values”, because
“If there were objective values, then they would be entities or qualities or relations of a very strange sort, utterly
different from anything else in the universe” (Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong, 1977, pp. 15, 38). Defend as
non-natural: Moore and the intuitionists.

• 3. So what does it mean to go pragmatist? A simple-minded pragmatist says (Blackburn):
• — Pose the external question about a stretch of talk; answer it by saying what is done by talking that way, not by
finding what it is about.

— Blackburn, “Pragmatism: All or Some?” (2013), p. 75: “You will be a pragmatist about an area of discourse if
you pose a Carnapian external question: how does it come about that we go in for this kind of discourse and
thought? [...] Instead, the explanation proceeds by talking in different terms of what is done by so talking.”

— Morality: the view was built for ethics first. Blackburn, p. 75, straight after the definition: “All this is entirely
in accord with the approach expressivists such as Gibbard and myself have taken to the ethical, which can
encompass the more general area of the ‘normative’ ”.

• 4. So: we should drop the demand to find something the discourse tracks; we should, instead, ask
what it does.

— Shanahan says this himself! On p. 42: “metaphysically flavoured questions about what consciousness is have
been supplanted by questions about how words like “consciousness”, “awareness”, “feeling”, and so on are used.”

— Morality: Price 1997, §4, says the same of moral talk: “the remaining puzzle is merely sociolinguistic, and not
metaphysical. It is the issue as to why creatures like us should find ourselves engaged in this particular linguistic
practice, not the issue as to what these moral properties are.”

• 5. What does the language do? Shanahan tells us:

— Prediction, p. 6: “To describe, predict, and explain complex behaviour, it often makes sense to adopt what
Dennett calls the intentional stance”.

— p. 33 fn 88: to extend the language of consciousness to something is to “call upon the resources of those language
games to describe the thing, to explain its past behaviour, to predict its future behaviour, and so on.”

— Fellowship and treatment, p. 33: the octopus “should be admitted to the fellowship of conscious creatures, and
treated accordingly.”

— Attitude, not opinion, p. 33: “we are not “of the opinion” that the child or the horse is a sentient being. Things
run much deeper than that. Unthinkingly, spontaneously, we simply take up the attitude towards them that we
adopt towards fellow conscious creatures.” (fn 89: PI II iv)

— Moral standing, p. 32: “the capacity for consciousness goes hand-in-hand with candidature for moral considera-
tion”.

— Morality: the expressivist’s answer for moral talk has the same shape: its function is “to give voice to certain
mental states, distinguished by their motivational role in human psychology” (Price 1997, §4). And the two
practices aren’t just parallel, they’re joined: admitting something to the fellowship of conscious creatures is
already a moral act (p. 32).

• 6. But then Shanahan looks for more than this:

— p. 10: “How is this space structured? What are its dimensions of variation, and where are its boundaries?”; the
“compendium” of specimens, like “19th century collectors”.

— As if there were an answer to be had apart from: should we extend these practices to this thing?

— He resists “Surely (the thought goes) there is a fact of the matter here.” (p. 8), but only by going to “nothing
IS anything” (p. 16) and “not ultimately real” (pp. 16-19, 25-27).

— When he does reach “should we extend?”, he puts it outside reason, p. 46: “the license to extend the language
of consciousness to AI cannot be witheld [sic] on empirical or rational grounds”; “So the decision whether to
extend the language of consciousness to AI is indeed a matter of existential orientation”,

— pp. 46-47: “we now set aside the concepts of truth, reality, rationality, etc, in favour of ambiguity, metaphor,
imagery, allegory, story, polysemy, paradox, contradiction, etc. There is no law of the excluded middle when it
comes to existential orientations.”

• 7. Diagnosis: Shanahan is hostage to the idea that language is representational.

— So, since there’s no hidden thing to represent, it must be that the represented thing is mysterious / empty.

— For this reason, a question with no represented thing behind it must be beyond reason!

— Morality: each of the moves in point 6 has a familiar moral twin:

* The specimen collection: the moral realist’s catalogue of the moral facts, as if there ‘were a list to be had
apart from what we should do.
* “Surely ... there is a fact of the matter here” (p. 8), answered with “not ultimately real”: Mackie’s error

* The capitals: “nothing IS anything” (p. 16) is the “metaphysically heavyweight conception of what it would

* 8. What Shanahan needs is therapy.

— Patient: the urge to keep looking after the answer is already in.

— And he cites the therapeutic Wittgenstein approvingly, as his own model (pp. 14, 56; p. 29 fns 79-81).

— Why, for a pragmatist of his sort, it’s a mistake to keep looking:

* Saying what the talk does (point 5) isn’t a first step towards the real story; it is the story. The account
“eschews any use of the referring expressions of the discourse” (Blackburn): there’s no second, deeper account
of what consciousness talk is about waiting behind it.

* So there’s nothing left to look for. A space of minds with “dimensions of variation” and “boundaries” (p. 10)
is exactly the subject matter the pragmatist said the talk doesn’t track. Mapping it is metaphysics coming
back in through the side door.

* And there’s nothing to lose by not finding it. Not finding it isn’t a discovery that consciousness is “empty”
or “not ultimately real” (pp. 16-19, 25-27); finding it was never what the talk was for. To be disappointed
is to have kept the representationalist’s expectations.

* What’s left are questions inside the practice: is the octopus in pain? should we admit this thing to the
fellowship? These get answered the ordinary way, with reasons. That is what “leaves everything as it is”
means: the fly is out of the bottle at point 5.

— Morality: this is exactly the quasi-realist’s point, and the moral case shows how it goes:

* Asked whether he only defends our right to talk “as if” there were moral truths: “No, no, no. I do not say
that we can talk as if kicking dogs were wrong, when ‘really’ it isn’t wrong. I say that it is wrong (so it is
true that it is wrong, so it is really true that it is wrong, so this is an example of a moral truth, so there
are moral truths).” (Blackburn, Ruling Passions, p. 319)

— Price 2013, p. 16 fn 5: “the subject naturalist recommends a dose of linguistic therapy: think carefully about what
you are assuming about language before you allow yourself to be convinced that there’s a genuine ontological
puzzle.”

— Blackburn, “Truth, Realism, and the Regulation of Theory”, Essays in Quasi-Realism (1993), p. 34: once the
quasi-realist has earned truth, knowledge and the rest, “The realist never intended to say more than this, nor
should the anti-realist have settled for less. [...] If this is so, philosophy indeed leaves everything as it is.”

— So I’m genuinely confused about why he wants to give us the things he is trying to give us: the map of the
space of possible minds (p. 10), the emptiness (“nothing IS anything”, p. 16), the existential orientation and the
poetry (pp. 46-47). By his own Wittgenstein there is nothing left to explain, and the discovery is the one that
lets us stop.

— His own reason for going on is the mistake, p. 30: “the post-reflective condition ought to be liberating, not a
shutting-down but an opening-out. Indeed, there are plenty of ways to respond to philosophy that treat it not
as a source of questions demanding answers, but as a call to the poetic.”

* That treats the pragmatist’s quiet as a shutting-down that something else has to open back up. But
pragmatism doesn’t shut down the conversation. It shuts down one question, the metaphysical one, and
leaves every other question standing.

* Is the octopus in pain? Should we admit it to the fellowship? Should we extend the language to an LLM,
given what it’s for, and what it costs to extend it wrongly or withhold it wrongly? All of that stays open,
and it is argued over with reasons, not handed to poetry.

* Morality: quasi-realism doesn’t end moral argument either. Once the question of moral truths “REALLY”
is dropped, we go on arguing about what’s wrong, exactly as before; that’s the point of earning “true” and
“really” inside the practice.

* So there was nothing to open out from. What is the opening-out for?

9. Why does he think he needs more? Two competing explanations.

— (a) He thinks the pragmatist story debunks. If consciousness talk is only for describing, predicting,
admitting to the fellowship and treating accordingly, then nothing backs it, so it is “not ultimately real” (pp. 16-
19, 25-27). He needs more to say where that leaves us, and what he finds is emptiness.

* The tell: for animistic talk he wants a third option, “neither that trees and rocks speak in exactly the way
that humans do, nor that they speak in a merely metaphorical sense (and hence, from a literal point of
view, do not really speak at all)” (p. 44, quoting Burley).

* The reply: that third option is quasi-realism. The “really” and the “true” are earned inside the practice; no
metaphysics is needed to vindicate it, or to empty it.

* Morality: this is the oldest objection to expressivism in ethics. Price 1997, §1: “Noncognitivism appeals
most in the moral case, but even here some feel that it devalues the talk it claims to save.”

— (b) He thinks the practice runs out at the edges. The language games grew up around embodied
creatures: for LLMs, “their lack of embodiment is especially challenging” (p. 42), and “the way LLMs are built
and deployed imposes structural constraints on what someone can coherently say about them” (p. 47). Where
the practice doesn’t settle “should we extend?”, he takes it that something beyond the practice must; finding
nothing, he hands the question to existential orientation, where “the jurisdiction of science and reason is limited”
(p. 46).

* The reply: an unsettled “should we extend?” is a practical question, and practical questions take reasons:

what the talk is for (point 5), and what it costs to extend it wrongly or withhold it wrongly. Not empirical,
not logical, but not beyond reason either. [Bottom line partly clipped in scan; reading uncertain.]
