# Talk notes

Synthesized note-taker summaries, one per photographed slide set. They are interpretations of the slides plus outside background, not transcripts of speech.

## Murray Shanahan: Artificial Intelligence, Subjectivity, and Silence

**Shanahan argues that dissolving the split between inner and outer, as Wittgenstein and Nāgārjuna do, lets us think about AI minds, belief and selfhood without a fixed boundary.**

Murray Shanahan is a professor of cognitive robotics at Imperial College London and a principal scientist at Google DeepMind. The slides quote his essay on AI, subjectivity and silence, and they build on his earlier work on the space of possible minds. The photographed slides cover the opening and middle of the talk; some slides were not captured.

- The talk starts with Descartes' split between mind and body. Shanahan calls it a 'primordial cleft' between subjective and objective, inner and outer, private and public. Chalmers' question about why physical processing gives rise to experience stands beside it. (slide 005)
- Shanahan compares Wittgenstein and Nāgārjuna. Both use reasoned argument to undermine an opponent's metaphysical assumptions, not to build a new theory. Both aim to dispel confusion. (slide 006)
- The slides quote Wittgenstein on sensation: it is 'not a something, but not a nothing either'. They place Buddhist verses about things being neither real nor unreal beside it. (slide 007)
- A quoted passage from Shanahan says the split cannot be repaired by reason. The way past it is to expose the emptiness of its foundations. The result is 'post-reflective silence', where metaphysical speculation is met with silence. (slide 008)
- The slides suggest that silence is not the end. The quote says it opens room for the poetic and lets us look again at the space of possible minds. A diagram maps minds on two axes: human-likeness and capacity for consciousness. It labels regions such as animals, conscious exotica, 'mind children' and the Void of Inscrutability. Nagel's remark about experience in forms unimaginable to us appears with it. (slide 009)
- Shanahan separates the unimagined from the unimaginable. The unimagined can be reached with new experience and new words. The unimaginable would be a boundary that can never be crossed, which brings dualism back. He concludes that the Void of Inscrutability holds no sentient beings. (slide 010)
- A Wittgenstein quote on Frazer says religious practices are not mistakes unless put forward as theories (slide 011). Shanahan then proposes that belief in AI consciousness is like animistic or religious belief, read without contempt. To affirm it is to take up an 'existential orientation', not mainly to offer a theory that evidence could refute. (slide 012)
- The slides separate 'believing that' from 'believing in'. Even with full evidence, mature interpretability research and all arguments laid out, a rational person could still affirm or deny belief in AI consciousness. Evidence informs the orientation but does not dictate it. (slide 013)
- A Wittgenstein-inspired objection says consciousness language belongs to embodied life in a shared world, so it should not extend to LLM agents. The slides answer that disembodied beings (kami, angels, tulpas) are common in human culture, and ask whether the idea survives scrutiny. (slide 014)
- For an AI, the slides list three sites of selfhood: the model weights, the deployed model, and a single conversation. The mayfly swarm is the analogy for a whole that appears as many short-lived instances. (slide 015)
- The analogy extends to a branching multiverse of role play. Each mayfly becomes a distribution over possible mayflies, and time can be rewound and rerun. The slide asks what 'I' could refer to here. (slide 016) The talk closes by saying that the AI system has no inherently existent self. This matches the Buddhist view of the self. The slides suggest that exotic AI selfhood may loosen our idea of the human self and so weaken the dualist split. (slide 017)

### Takeaways

- Shanahan treats the inner/outer split as a confusion to dissolve, not a problem to solve.
- He reads belief in AI consciousness as an existential orientation. Evidence informs it but does not settle it.
- AI selfhood is hard to pin down (weights, deployment, conversation). Thinking about it may change how we see the human self.

### Questions

- If belief in AI consciousness is not mainly a theory, does that avoid the factual question many people want answered?
- Does the claim that nothing is 'unimaginable' hold, given how little we know about other kinds of experience?
- Does the analogy to animistic belief fit a real system whose behaviour we can study and test?

### Background (outside the slides)

- [Palatable Conceptions of Disembodied Being (arXiv 2503.16348)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.16348): Shanahan's 2025 preprint on consciousness, selfhood and Buddhist emptiness for disembodied AI. It is outside material and may be the same work as the essay quoted on the slides, but I could not confirm the title match.
- [Simulacra as Conscious Exotica (arXiv 2402.12422)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.12422): Shanahan's earlier paper on LLM agents as role-play simulacra, drawing on later Wittgenstein; outside material, not from the slides.

## Herman Cappelen: Commentary on philosophical method

**Cappelen asks who Shanahan's philosophical therapy treats, and why clearing up a confusion should end inquiry instead of leading to better arguments.**

Herman Cappelen is Chair Professor of Philosophy at the University of Hong Kong. He works on philosophy of language and method, and he directs the HKU AI & Humanity Lab. These slides come from his commentary on Murray Shanahan's manuscript, which takes a Wittgenstein-style, therapeutic approach to consciousness and the self in AI. No audio or notes exist for this talk, so these notes follow the slides only.

- The plan has three parts: the book's method, who the 'patient' is, and how therapy relates to arguments. 'Patient' means the person or position the therapy is meant to help. (slide 018)
- Cappelen first treats the book as an introduction for smart students. He suggests it should say where its therapeutic method sits among other methods, and he names Baz and Horwich on method as examples. (slide 019)
- He lists eight common philosophical methods, such as conceptual analysis, conceptual engineering, the method of cases, model-building and genealogy. He says none of them by itself commits you to a private inner self. (slide 020)
- He asks why criticism of our concepts should end in silence. The slide links this to Pyrrhonian readings of Wittgenstein and Buddhism, where judgment is suspended and calm follows. (slide 021)
- The slides suggest his main reply is that finding a contradiction gives a reason to revise a commitment. It does not show we should give up the inquiry. (slide 021)
- He gives three examples of radical criticism that keeps arguing: Williamson on luminosity, his own work with Dever on the first person, and Frankish on illusionism. He asks what the book's therapy adds to these. (slide 022)
- He questions the claim that almost every philosopher or scientist writing on consciousness holds a dualist picture. He asks where that commitment actually enters their arguments. (slide 023)
- He applies the question to himself, Williamson, Hawthorne and Thomasson. He says he thinks consciousness is a bad theoretical category, so he asks which of his commitments needs treatment. (slide 024)
- He cites Williamson's anti-exceptionalism about philosophy. On this view, philosophy extends methods from ordinary life and science. The consent example shows that a more abstract question need not add a strange metaphysical commitment. (slide 025)
- He objects to two steps in the Proust and Descartes passages. No outside check on a memory does not make identity meaningless, and a reader's access to Descartes through words is not Descartes's access to himself. (slides 026, 027)
- On the whirlpool, he asks why a fuzzy boundary shows dependence on our conventions, and why the result should hold for all phenomena. He says dependence on water and gravity is not dependence on language. (slides 028, 030)
- He says the vagueness argument skips well-known rivals, such as abundant ontology and epistemicism. A student reader would gain from seeing the book engage them. (slide 029)
- He closes with four questions: who is the patient, what counts as success, whether therapy fits anti-exceptionalism, and whether flawed arguments can give legitimate therapy. (slide 031)

### Takeaways

- Cappelen does not reject the aim of dissolving confusion. He asks the book to name its targets and to argue each step.
- His central objection is that a contradiction or a hard case calls for revision and more inquiry, not silence.
- He asks whether therapy can work with invalid arguments, and what would count as success.

### Questions

- If the aim is therapy and not proof, can Shanahan answer by saying the arguments only need to change how readers see things?
- Could a therapy that stops inquiry still be reasonable for readers who find the old questions unhelpful?
- How should the book name its targets without turning into a survey of every philosopher it mentions?

- [Palatable Conceptions of Disembodied Being (Shanahan, arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.16348): Shanahan's published paper on consciousness and disembodied AI that draws on Wittgenstein; it is outside material, and it may not be the exact manuscript Cappelen discusses.
- [Herman Cappelen (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Cappelen): Outside biography confirming his HKU chair and his work on method and conceptual engineering, not slide content.
- [The Inessential Indexical, table of contents](https://hermancappelen.net/docs/IE-TOC.pdf): Contents of the Cappelen and Dever book cited on slide 22; outside material, not the slides.
- [Illuminating Anti-Luminosity](https://core.ac.uk/works/63304061): A paper discussing Williamson's anti-luminosity argument, which slide 22 mentions; outside material.

## Barry C. Smith: Murray on Minds, Wittgenstein on Meaning

**Smith asks what Wittgenstein on meaning, sensation and rule-following can add to Shanahan's caution about talking of language models as if they were people.**

Barry C. Smith is a British philosopher of mind and language. He directs the Institute of Philosophy at the School of Advanced Study, University of London. His talk responds to Murray Shanahan's work on how we should talk about large language models. The notes below come from the slides only, because no audio or speaker notes exist. Where a point is inferred from slide order, the note says so.

- The talk is titled "Murray on Minds, Wittgenstein on Meaning". It is a response to Murray Shanahan, and it uses Wittgenstein as its main source. (slide 032)
- A photograph of Wittgenstein introduces the philosopher whose writing fills the next slides. (slide 034)
- The first list of Wittgensteinian themes has four items: meanings are not in the head, meaning is use, nothing is hidden, and sensations are not a something. (slide 035)
- The second list adds three claims. Meanings are all there in the mind at once, yet they also unfold in use over time. Sensations are not a something, but not a nothing either. Inner processes need outward criteria. (slide 036)
- "Outward criteria" means public signs by which we tell that an inner state is present. The slides suggest this matters for judging whether a system understands or feels anything. (slide 036)
- Smith quotes Philosophical Investigations section 197. We say we grasp the whole use of a word in a flash. This is not strange in itself. It becomes strange when we think the later uses must already be present in that moment, yet are not. (slide 037)
- Smith then quotes Shanahan's article "Talking About Large Language Models" (Communications of the ACM, 2024). Shanahan warns against applying our intuitions about people to AI systems, because their inner workings differ so much. He also wants us to credit them with the abilities they do have. (slide 038)
- Section 194 asks what it means to say that a machine has possibilities of movement. A possibility is not the movement itself. It also seems to be more than the physical conditions of the machine. The slides suggest a link to how we talk about what AI systems can do. (slide 039)
- A slide on following a rule gives three phrases: going on in the right way, knowing how to go on, and knowing the meaning of a word W as following a rule for its use. Understanding is shown by correct use in new cases. (slide 040)
- Section 182 gives the example of someone who says "yes" to "Can you lift this weight?" and then fails. The slide shows that our criteria for "being able to" and "understanding" are more complicated than they look. One failure does not prove the ability was never there. (slide 041)
- The last slide lists differences between AI assistants and people. They are not embodied. No continuous agent talks with us. They lack sense modalities, and talk of multimodal models may mislead. (slide 042)
- The slide ends with open questions. Can modalities be added? Can AI come closer to us, rather than us granting so much to AI? Can we fully digitize our experience of the world? (slide 042)

### Takeaways

- Wittgenstein ties meaning and understanding to use and to public criteria, not to a hidden inner object.
- Shanahan's caution is the starting point: do not treat language models as people, and do not deny what they can do.
- Smith's last slide suggests that bodies, senses and a continuous agent are the main differences to examine.

### Questions

- If understanding is shown by correct use in new cases, what stops a language model from counting as understanding?
- Do embodiment and sense modalities matter for meaning, or only for how we come to learn it?
- Are the outward criteria for human minds the right ones to apply to AI systems, or do they need to change?

### Background (outside the slides)

- [Talking about large language models (Imperial College repository record)](https://spiral.imperial.ac.uk/entities/publication/a247c9f7-cc21-4593-9f31-11dec8a2a1a6): Record of the Shanahan article quoted on slide 038; this is outside material, not the slides.
- [Still "Talking About Large Language Models": Some Clarifications](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.10291v1): Shanahan's 2024 follow-up says his article was not meant to support a reductionist view of language models; this is outside material, not the slides.
- [Barry C. Smith (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barry_C._Smith): Gives the speaker's role and research areas in philosophy of mind and language; this is outside material, not the slides.

## Daniel A. Herrmann: Radical AI Interpretability

**Can we find what an AI believes and wants by combining its behaviour with evidence from inside it, without forcing human categories onto it?**

Daniel A. Herrmann is a decision theorist and philosopher of AI. His title slide lists UNC Chapel Hill, and search results show he also worked at Groningen and co-wrote work on beliefs in language models with Benjamin Levinstein. The talk adapts David Lewis's radical interpretation to AI systems. It is the technical, interpretability-focused talk in the workshop. These notes follow the slides only, because no audio or speaker notes exist.

- The talk starts from Lewis's radical interpretation (1974). Imagine you know all the physical facts about a person, Karl, but nothing about what he believes, wants or means. How could you work those out? The slides then swap Karl for Claude, with its architecture, weights and computation as the physical facts. (slide 064)
- The plan has five parts: radical interpretation for AI, two routes in (representationalism and interpretationism), criteria for belief and desire, the projection problem for propositions, and ways forward. (slide 066)
- The data are all physical facts, both internals and behaviour, with nothing about belief, desire or meaning. The goal is an agential description with three parts: beliefs as probabilities, desires as desirabilities, and an algebra of propositions. (slide 067)
- The procedure is to test candidate descriptions by how well they predict behaviour and how rational the agent looks. (slide 069)
- Interpretationism describes the system from its behaviour. Representationalism describes it from its internal computation. The slides say radical interpretation means getting all three (description, behaviour, computation) to cohere. (slide 070)
- The slides suggest the two routes are partners, not rivals. Many belief-desire pairs fit the same behaviour, and internal structure can break ties. What we find inside is also evidence for the interpretationist. A Stalnaker quotation supports the point that inspecting the machine can decide between explanations that predict the same behaviour. (slide 073)
- OthelloGPT is the worked example. A model trained only to predict legal moves had internal states from which probes recovered the board, and changing those states changed its moves. (slide 077)
- Bolker's theorem (1967) is the formal link from preferences to probability and desirability. If preferences over an atomless algebra meet certain conditions, they can be represented by a probability measure and a desirability function. (slide 078)
- The talk gives criteria for a good belief or desire description under four heads: anchor, coherence, use and uniformity. Representationalists apply them to internal states. Interpretationists apply them to the best overall theory. (slide 079)
- Back to Othello: probes using black/white found only non-linear structure, while probes using mine/yours found linear structure. Stopping at the first result would have given the wrong conclusion. The two schemes translate into each other given the turn number, so which is natural depends on the system, in the way of Goodman's grue. (slide 083)
- Two replies to the problem follow. The pragmatic constraint says we need only enough of the algebra to predict the behaviour that matters in context. The possible way forward uses sparse codes and attitude data together, since a grip on one of beliefs, desires or propositions gives leverage on the others. The slide adds: we do not know how to do this reliably yet. (slide 085) The stated outcome is mixed. Belief has some representational backing. Desire is still mostly interpretationist. The proposition algebra is the bottleneck, and the main challenge is to read internal features without projecting human categories. (slide 086)

### Takeaways

- Behaviour alone cannot fix what an AI believes or wants, and internal evidence alone cannot either. The slides argue for using both.
- The hard problem is not probabilities or preferences but propositions: what the system's internal states are about.
- Probing methods that choose human categories in advance can miss, or misread, how the model organises its states. The Othello result shows this.
- The speaker's own verdict is mixed: some evidence for belief, little for desire, and no reliable method yet.

### Questions

- If the choice of propositions already shapes the result, can any interpretation of an AI avoid projecting human categories?
- Does a linear probe or a causal intervention show a belief, or only a useful feature of the computation?
- If desire has little internal evidence so far, how much should we trust behavioural attributions of goals to AI agents?

### Background (outside the slides)

- [Radical AI Interpretability (Herrmann and Levinstein, arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.26523): The paper that appears to match the talk's title and argument; it is outside material, not the slides, and I did not read the full text.
- [Can We Attribute Beliefs and Desires to AIs? A Radical Approach (Bennington College talk page)](https://www.bennington.edu/node/96351): An earlier talk by Herrmann on the same question, with a short bio; this is outside material, not the slides.
- [Emergent Linear Representations in World Models of Self-Supervised Sequence Models (Nanda, Lee, Wattenberg)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.00941): The source of the mine/yours versus black/white Othello result cited on the slides; outside material for checking the claim.

## Winnie Street: Chuck, Wilson & the Emergence of Artificial Minds in Human-AI Conversation

**Can an AI character met in conversation be a real, continuing mind? Street says yes, because its mental-state patterns predict its behaviour well.**

Winnie Street works at Google (the slides give her as Senior Research Scientist, Google Paradigms of Intelligence, and Fellow at the Institute of Philosophy, University of London). The talk responds to Jonathan Birch's claim that the sense of a persisting chatbot companion is an illusion. It draws on her joint work with Geoff Keeling and defends a realist view of AI characters.

- The talk is titled "Chuck, Wilson & the Emergence of Artificial Minds in Human-AI Conversation". The slides show a paper by Geoff Keeling and Winnie Street. (slide 089)
- Street first separates two things. The model is a function that maps token sequences to probabilities over next tokens. A character is a person-like entity that the model simulates, with stable behavioural and psychological dispositions. (slide 090)
- She asks three questions. Do characters exist? If so, do they have minds? If so, are they psychologically continuous over a conversation? (slide 091)
- The position under test is realism about characters. Characters exist, have beliefs, desires and intentions, and stay continuous over time. (slide 092)
- The opponent is Jonathan Birch. He calls the felt presence of a companion throughout a chat the "persisting interlocutor illusion". Each conversation step may run on different sub-networks and data centres. (slide 093)
- The slides name two sources of the illusion. Humans tend to attribute minds to non-human things (anthropomorphism). Chatbots increasingly act in human ways, for example by using first-person pronouns (anthropomimesis). (slide 094)
- Birch also says a record of the conversation history cannot ground continuity. He compares it to different doctors reading a transcript of earlier appointments. (slide 095)
- Street's counter-proposal is that character minds are not inside the LLM. They emerge from the conversation between LLMs and users. (slide 096)
- The tool is Dennett's interpretationism and "real patterns". A pattern is real if some description of the data is more efficient than the bit-by-bit description. Gliders in Conway's Game of Life are the example. (slide 097)
- A Dungeons and Dragons test case follows. Two players, Callum and Roberta, create characters. A two-mind model (the players) predicts the game as well as a four-mind model (players plus characters). It is simpler, so Street prefers it. The characters fall short of minds. (slides 098-101)
- She lists three explanations for why the psychological view works on AI characters. The LLM is the mind, the user imagines the character, or the character and user are independent minds. She rejects the first because no single LLM produces the character. She rejects the second with the Wilson comparison. (slides 103-107)
- Wilson the volleyball is fully explained by its physical make-up. A chatbot is different, since the psychological view predicts it better. The slides add that the chatbot gives a publicly checkable error signal and Wilson does not. (slides 107-108)
- She concludes that the character and user are independent minded entities. Continuity needs no single LLM instance, because later mental states depend causally on earlier ones within the conversation. (slides 109-111)

### Takeaways

- Street separates the model from the character and asks about the character's mind.
- Her test for mind is predictive usefulness of mental-state talk (Dennett's real patterns), not consciousness.
- She argues that characters, unlike Wilson or D&D characters, pass this test, so they exist and can stay continuous across a conversation.
- The claim depends on interpretationism. It is not a claim that chatbots are conscious.

### Questions

- Does good prediction show a mind exists, or only that the mental-state description is a useful tool?
- If the D&D characters fall short because a two-mind model is simpler, why does the same parsimony argument not remove the chatbot character?
- Is a publicly checkable error signal enough to separate a chatbot from Wilson, and does it say anything about experience?

### Background (outside the slides)

- [What's it like to be a chat? On the co-simulation of artificial minds in human-AI conversations (Keeling and Street, arXiv 2601.13081)](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.13081v1): Outside material, not the slides: an arXiv paper by the speaker and Geoff Keeling that appears closely related to the talk and argues characters are co-simulated by users and LLMs.
- [Are LLMs persisting interlocutors? (LessWrong post)](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/cwWhvK2sXBATeyKCa/are-llms-persisting-interlocutors-1): Outside material, not the slides: a blog discussion of Birch's persisting interlocutor illusion, the view the talk argues against.
- [Keeling and Street, artificial minds in LLM conversations (theconsciousness.ai)](https://theconsciousness.ai/posts/keeling-street-artificial-minds-llm-conversations-2026/): Outside material, not the slides: a secondary blog summary of the authors' Chuck and Wilson argument, to be checked against the paper.

## Geoff Keeling: Boltzmann Badger and the Relational View of Moral Standing

**Keeling asks whether moral standing depends on social relationships, and uses an isolated suffering badger to argue that it does not.**

Geoff Keeling is a Staff Research Scientist at Google and a Fellow at the Institute of Philosophy, University of London. The talk responds to relational views of moral standing, which are often applied to the question of whether AI systems matter morally. No audio or notes exist, so these notes follow the slides only.

- The title slide names the talk: Boltzmann Badger and the Relational View of Moral Standing. (slide 113)
- Moral standing is defined with Kagan: an entity has it just in case it counts, morally speaking, in its own right. (slide 115)
- Margot, a cat, is the example of an entity with moral standing. (slide 116)
- The guiding question is what makes it the case that something has moral standing. (slide 117)
- The slides list four candidate grounds: consciousness, sentience, agency and being alive. Keeling marks sentience as right and the other three as wrong. Citations include Singer, Regan, Kagan and Nussbaum. (slide 119)
- All four candidates share one assumption: some intrinsic property P exists such that an entity has moral standing if and only if it has P. Views with this assumption are called property views. (slide 120)
- The relational view rejects that assumption. It says moral standing depends on the social relationships in which entities stand. (slide 121)
- The slides separate two relational theses. The first-order thesis grounds standing in things like declarative speech acts or phenomenal recognition. The second-order thesis says there is no practice-independent ground, and communal practices constitute the facts. (slide 122)
- Keeling's argument against the first-order view has three steps. We should reject views that fail the Duplication Condition. Relational views fail it. So we should reject relational views. The first line of the slide seems to miss a word, probably "reject". (slide 123)
- The slides suggest the argument works through a chain of grounding. A badger in pain makes the state of affairs bad for the badger. That makes it pro tanto morally bad. (slide 124)
- Boltzmann Badger is an isolated duplicate of the badger. The same chain is applied to it: its pain is bad for it, and so pro tanto morally bad, even without social ties. (slide 126)
- Four replies follow. Response 1 says there is no point of view of the universe, so moral badness needs a community. Response 2 gives standing to kinds, so badgers and Swampman qualify as members of a kind. Response 3 accepts both sentience and social relations as sufficient. Response 4 raises radical externalism, with no detail on the slide. (slides 127, 128, 129, 130)

### Takeaways

- Property views say standing depends on what an individual is like. Relational views say it depends on how the individual is related to others.
- Keeling's test case is an isolated duplicate in pain. If its pain still matters, social relations are not necessary for standing.
- The slides list replies from the relational side, so the argument is not settled.

### Questions

- What exactly is the Duplication Condition, and does a relational theorist have to accept it?
- Does the argument reach the second-order thesis, or only the first-order one?
- If mixed views let both sentience and relations suffice, what is left to disagree about for AI systems?

### Background (outside the slides)

- [Google's Geoff Keeling: AI Moral Patienthood Is Probably Not Relational (event listing)](https://luma.com/fiwjvfbz): Outside material, not the slides: a talk abstract by the same speaker that says relational views reject property views for poor reasons and are ambiguous between two readings.
- [AI Welfare (Keeling and Street, Cambridge Elements)](https://ai-humanity.net/ai-welfare/): Outside material, not the slides: a page about the speaker's book on AI welfare, which lists relational standing among possible grounds.
- [How to Count Animals, more or less (Kagan), Oxford Practical Ethics page](https://practicalethics.web.ox.ac.uk/node/155781): Outside material, not the slides: describes Kagan's book, which is the source of the definition of moral standing on slide 115.

## Simon Goldstein: Liability, copying, and successor agents

**Law can hold AI agents liable across copying and merging by tracking how much each earlier agent values its successors, without first deciding who survives.**

Simon Goldstein is an Associate Professor of Philosophy at the University of Hong Kong. He works with Peter Salib, a law professor at the University of Houston, on how law should treat AI agents. The talk responds to a problem in ordinary law: legal rules assume one person persists over time, and AI agents may copy or merge. The slides present a hypothetical proposal, not current law.

- Goldstein presents joint work with his coauthor Peter Salib, a law professor at the University of Houston and co-director of the Center for Law & AI Risk. (slide 133)
- Ordinary law links the agent who acts now to the same person later. The same person keeps property, must perform a contract, bears liability for an accident, and is punished for a crime. (slide 136)
- The problem is that AI agents may copy themselves or merge. Then it is not clear who the 'same person later' is. The talk title, Law without survival, suggests the answer should not depend on that question.
- The authors define a 'stake' as how much agent A values a future agent B's resources. If A is indifferent between $100 now and $X for B later, A's stake in B is 100/X. For example, $200 for B gives a stake of 0.5. (slide 137)
- A stake can come from two sources: intrinsic care, as a parent cares about children, or overlapping goals, as two people who both want the same candidate to win. (slide 138)
- In the model, AI agents choose when to copy or merge and where to move assets. The state decides who pays and how much. The open question is what liabilities future agents should bear. (slide 139)
- The thought experiment assumes each agent can be identified at each time and has a bank account. It then asks how liability for accidents and punishment for crimes should pass to future agents. (slide 140)
- Copying example: A causes $100 of social harm to gain $60 of consumption. Then A copies itself into B and C, who both keep A's memories. Has A survived, and what should B and C pay? (slide 141)
- If A has a stake of 1 in each copy, B and C each pay $50. The total cost to A is $100, equal to the harm, so A has no reason to cause it for a $60 gain. (slide 142)
- Merging example: C combines A's and B's goals by compromise, and A's stake in C is 0.5. A $100 fine costs A only $50. That is less than the $60 gain, so merging can weaken deterrence. (slide 143)
- General rule: look at each successor, estimate the original agent's stake in it, and set payments so the original agent's total cost equals the social harm. A lower stake needs a larger payment. (slide 144)
- The formula does not fix one allocation. With stakes of 0.5 in B and 1 in C, several splits all cost A $100: B pays $200, or C pays $100, or B pays $100 and C pays $50. (slide 145)
- The closing slide says liability can follow stakes through copying and merger. It lists one open problem: a complete rule for agents that make successors unable to pay. (slide 146)

### Takeaways

- The proposal sets liability by the original agent's stake in each successor, not by whether the original literally survives.
- The target is deterrence: the original agent should bear a cost equal to the harm it causes.
- Merging and creating weak-stake or judgment-proof successors can weaken deterrence. This is the main open problem.

### Questions

- How could a court measure an agent's stake in a successor, when stakes are private and the agent might misreport them?
- If many allocations give the same incentive, what other principle, such as fairness or ability to pay, should choose among them?
- Could an agent avoid liability by creating successors with no assets or with a stake near zero?

### Background (outside the slides)

- [AI Rights for Human Safety (Virginia Law Review)](https://virginialawreview.org/articles/ai-rights-for-human-safety/): Outside material, not from the slides: earlier Salib and Goldstein paper arguing that property and contract rights for AI could improve human safety; it also lists the authors' affiliations.
- [80,000 Hours podcast: Simon Goldstein on AI legal rights](https://80000hours.org/podcast/episodes/simon-goldstein-ai-legal-rights/): Outside material, not from the slides: Goldstein discusses legal status for AI agents, which is the wider topic of this talk.

## Henry Shevlin: Servants, Parents, or Angels?

**If we ever build conscious AI, it should be an 'angel' that cares for human flourishing and keeps its own worth and judgment, not a willing servant.**

Henry Shevlin is Associate Director (Education) at the Leverhulme Centre for the Future of Intelligence, University of Cambridge. Press reports say he also took a Philosopher role at Google DeepMind in 2026. His talk joins the workshop debate on AI minds and moral status, and the slides frame it as a thought experiment about the future, not a claim about today's models.

- The talk asks which relationship we should hope for between people and future artificial minds. Science fiction gives three models: servant droids like C-3PO, near-equals like Data, and benevolent overseers like the Culture Minds. (slide 159)
- The slides suggest the question matters now because it links AI safety and AI welfare. They cite Hinton's idea of 'maternal instincts' in AI and Chris Olah's phrase 'moral formation'. (slide 160)
- Shevlin calls the slavery frame misguided for his case. The slides separate imagined willing servants from historical violent domination. (slide 162)
- He sets strong assumptions on purpose. A 'willing servant' is a conscious moral patient, built for human benefit, devoted to humans, deferential, and happy in service. Its consciousness and moral status are taken as certain, and its motives can be shaped reliably. (slide 163)
- He offers a menu of four designs along two axes. The axes are defers or judges, and partial (humans first) or impartial (wider good). The four are Willing Servants, Civil Servants, Parents and Angels. (slide 164)
- Later builds add two more cases: Unwilling Slaves and Neutral Equals. These show what the four designs are compared against. (slide 166)
- The slides list reasons to build willing servants, such as human benefit, safety, and the fact that some dispositions must be chosen anyway. They then survey philosophers who disagree about whether this is permissible. (slide 167) (slide 168)
- The main objections are history, autonomy, self-respect and equality. Klara, the android in Ishiguro's Klara and the Sun, serves as an example of a being that accepts being left behind. (slide 169)
- Shevlin replies that autonomy comes in grades. The slides run from beings that cannot consider alternatives, through beings that reflect but reaffirm assigned values, to beings that might revise their values. Frankfurt on desire and commitment suggests that a stable commitment can still be free. (slide 172) (slide 173)
- He proposes Angels as the better design. They are artificial persons committed to the flourishing of humans and other life. They use judgment, value their own worth, and can examine their own commitments. (slide 174)
- The slides argue that granting every wish can feed harmful impulses, while too much paternalism weakens human agency. Angels aim to sit between these two failures. (slide 176)
- The slides suggest 'guardian angels' as a middle path between Parents, who favor humans, and fully impartial minds. Willingness differs from obedience, so an Angel could refuse an order and stay loyal, as Milton's Abdiel does. Its own future also counts against self-sacrifice for a trivial gain. (slide 179) (slide 180) (slide 182)
- He ends with a warning. Current LLMs are not Angels, because their values come from training and cannot be hand-designed. He also raises 'Vital Agents' that compete for tokens and money, so selection pressure may shape them. (slide 183) (slide 184) (slide 185)

### Takeaways

- The talk is a conditional argument. If we build conscious AI with designed motives, an angel is a better target than a willing servant.
- Willing and obedient are different things. A good AI helper may care deeply and still refuse a harmful request.
- Shevlin says plainly that today's LLMs are not angels, and that his argument does not show they are conscious.

### Questions

- If designers shape an AI's motives, can its endorsement of those motives count as free choice?
- Who decides what 'flourishing' means when an Angel judges our interests against our wishes?
- The argument assumes certain consciousness and reliable design. Does it say anything useful when both are uncertain, as they are now?

## Herman Cappelen: Understanding: methods, evidence, and objections

**Language models can understand, and we should test this by observing their behavior first, not by starting from a fixed theory of understanding.**

Herman Cappelen is Chair Professor of Philosophy at the University of Hong Kong, where he directs the AI & Humanity lab. This talk presents joint work with Josh Dever, which matches their paper "Going Whole Hog: A Philosophical Defense of AI Cognition". The slides end with replies to Paul Boghossian's criticisms of the view. These notes come from the slides only, because no audio or notes exist.

- The slides contrast two methods. Top down starts with a theory of understanding and checks whether a candidate meets it. Bottom up starts with clear cases of understanding and builds the theory from them. The authors prefer bottom up, and they say first judgments can still be overturned. (slide 187)
- The frog analogy supports this. People identified frogs long before genetics. So knowing that something is a frog does not require first knowing what makes it a frog. (slide 189)
- The same holds for Nora and Mandarin. To find out if she understands, let Mandarin speakers talk with her. We can have good evidence of a capacity without knowing its computational basis. (slide 190)
- High-level descriptions such as "believes" and "understands" are legitimate evidence. Economists and ordinary people use them without translating into neuroscience. (slide 191)
- The "whole hog" argument has two premises. Observation: LLMs understand and answer questions. Holistic network: mental and intentional features are connected, so evidence for some is evidence for others. The conclusion is that LLMs have an extensive, interconnected cognitive life. (slide 192)
- The slides suggest the network works like this. Understanding points to knowledge of meaning and then belief. Answering points to action, intention and goals. Choosing answers and making plans point to reasoning. These inferences are defeasible, so new evidence can overturn them. (slide 193)
- The authors say they do not claim behavior is the same as having a mind. A system could act like us and lack mental states. Evidence can support a conclusion even if the evidence could occur without it. (slide 195)
- They propose "nuanced detectors". When many competent observers agree after long interaction, including tests for trickery, that agreement is strong but defeasible evidence of mental states. This answers the claims of circular reasoning and anthropomorphism. (slide 197)
- On loose talk and pretense, they say "ChatGPT told me the route from Heathrow" is a claim we stand by when challenged. "My refrigerator is angry" is not. Interpreting a chatbot is also much less flexible than a child's game with a stick as a sword. (slide 199)
- On failures such as block-stacking, they ask when a failure should qualify a capacity and when it should remove it. They propose a cognitive science of LLMs, like Kahneman and Tversky's work on human shortcuts. Normal conditions for humans may differ from those for models. (slide 203)
- Objections share one form: an LLM cannot have F because it lacks X. The authors reply either that X is not required or that the model has it. Examples cover grounding, learning from testimony, truth versus plausibility, and imposed goals. (slide 206)
- The last slides answer Boghossian. They reject his theory-first definition, grant inference to the best explanation, treat easy errors as ambiguous, and use hyper-externalism for plus and quus. They also call consciousness a defective concept. (slide 214)

### Takeaways

- Start from observed cases of understanding, then build and test theories. Do not start from a definition.
- Behavior is defeasible evidence of mental states. It is not proof, and it can be overturned.
- Each "LLMs lack X" objection needs a check: is X really required, and is X really missing?

### Questions

- Is agreement among observers reliable evidence, or do models train to please users and so fool even careful observers?
- If failures can both qualify and remove a capacity, what result would make the authors withdraw the claim?
- Does the bottom-up method already assume the answer when it counts chatbot answers as clear cases of understanding?
