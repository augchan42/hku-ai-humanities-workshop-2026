## Summary of the photographed set

Smith brings Wittgenstein’s ideas about meaning, sensations, and following rules into a discussion of Shanahan’s work. His slides contrast learning to use a word with imagining that every future use must already be present in a person’s mind. They also raise questions about how human conversations and language models differ, including embodiment and sensory contact with the world. The photographed slides give an outline, not a complete record of the spoken argument.

## 037 — Philosophical Investigations §197

### Visible text

197. “It’s as if we could grasp the whole use of a word in a flash.”—And that is just what we say we do. That is to say: we sometimes describe what we do in these words. But there is nothing astonishing, nothing queer, about what happens. It becomes queer when we are led to think that the future development must in some way already be present in the act of grasping the use and yet isn’t present.—For we say that there isn’t any doubt that we understand the word, and on the other hand its meaning lies in its use.

### In everyday language

A new idea can seem to click all at once. The passage asks whether that requires every later application of the word to be somehow packed into that instant. The quotation contrasts the experience of understanding with the unfolding practice of using a word.

## 039 — Philosophical Investigations §194

### Visible text

194. The kind of way in which we talk about machines. We say, for example, that a machine has (possesses) such-and-such possibilities of movement; we speak of the ideally rigid machine which can only move in such-and-such a way.—What is this possibility of movement? It is not the movement, but it does not seem to be the mere physical conditions for moving either.

### In everyday language

We can say a machine *can* move in a certain way even when it is standing still. The passage asks what we mean by that possibility: it is different from the actual movement, and the relationship to the machine’s physical setup needs explanation.

## 041 — Philosophical Investigations §182, item 5

### Visible text

182. 5) Someone asks me “Can you lift this weight?” I answer “Yes”. Now he says “Do it!”—and I can’t. In what kind of circumstances would it count as a justification to say “When I answered ‘yes’ I could do it, only now I can’t”? The criteria which we accept for ‘fitting’, ‘being able to’, ‘understanding’, are much more complicated than might appear at first sight.

### In everyday language

Failing once does not always prove that someone never had an ability: perhaps circumstances changed, or they were tired. The example suggests that our judgments about ability and understanding depend on more context than a single success or failure.
