# Geoff Keeling — Boltzmann Badger and Moral Standing

## Summary

Keeling asks what makes an individual matter morally. He contrasts accounts based on an individual’s own properties with accounts based on social relationships, then uses an imagined isolated badger in pain to challenge a relational view. The photographed slides also set out possible replies; the positions are philosophical arguments, not a settled consensus.

## 120 — A shared assumption

### Visible text

(Shared Assumption) There exists some intrinsic property P such that an entity x has moral standing if and only if Px.

Call any view which implies the Shared Assumption a property view of moral standing.

### In everyday language

A property view says something about the individual itself determines whether it has moral standing. P is a placeholder for a proposed feature, such as sentience.

## 121 — The relational view

### Visible text

The relational view rejects the Shared Assumption.

It holds that moral standing depends on the social relationships in which entities stand.

### In everyday language

A relational approach instead places weight on relationships and social interaction. The talk examines this proposed alternative.

## 122 — Two relational theses

### Visible text

1. First-Order Grounding Thesis: Moral standing is grounded not in intrinsic properties, but in, inter alia, declarative speech acts or phenomenal recognition.

2. Second-Order Thesis: There is no practice-independent ground of moral standing; our communal practices constitute the facts about moral standing.

### In everyday language

The first proposal ties standing to things such as recognition by others; the second says communal practices help make the moral facts what they are. These are different versions of a relationship-centered view.

## 123 — Argument against the first-order view

### Visible text

My argument against the first-order relational view

1. We should all views which fail the Duplication Condition.
2. Relational views fail the Duplication Condition.
3. So, we should reject relational views.

### In everyday language

The slide appears to omit a word in its first sentence; it is reproduced as shown. Keeling argues that a theory should treat relevant duplicates consistently and that the targeted relational account fails that test.

## 124 — A badger in pain

### Visible text

[Descriptive State of Affairs] Badger is in excruciating pain.

Grounds

[Prudential Evaluative Fact] The state of affairs is Bad for Badger.

Grounds

[Moral Evaluative Fact] The state of affairs is pro tanto morally bad.

### In everyday language

The proposed chain runs from a badger suffering, to that suffering being bad for the badger, to there being a moral reason against it. “Pro tanto” means a reason that counts even if other considerations might also matter.

## 127 — Response 1: moral community

### Visible text

Response 1: The Point of View of the Universe is Weird

Pain is prudentially bad for Boltzmann Badger, but social relationships are an enabling condition on pain grounding moral badness.

There is no Sidgwickian “point of view of the universe” — the moral point of view arises only within communities.

### In everyday language

One possible response accepts that pain is bad for the badger but says moral evaluation requires a community. The slide presents this as a reply to the objection, not necessarily Keeling’s own view.

## 128 — Response 2: kinds

### Visible text

Response 2: Species Kinds

Moral standing is conferred on kinds rather than individuals.

Boltzmann Badger has moral standing because moral standing is held by badgers tout court (and Swampman because he is human).

### In everyday language

Another possible reply gives standing to members of a kind, such as badgers or humans, even when a particular individual lacks ordinary relationships. Swampman is another philosophical thought experiment.

## 129 — Response 3: mixed views

### Visible text

Response 3: Mixed Disjunctive Views

Concede that intrinsic properties like sentience are sufficient for moral standing, but hold that social relations are also sufficient.

Then Boltzmann Badger and Swampman have standing, while a socially embedded non-sentient robot has standing and an isolated duplicate robot does not.

### In everyday language

A mixed view allows more than one route to moral standing: having a relevant capacity or having particular social relationships. It can therefore give different answers for two otherwise similar robots in different social settings.
