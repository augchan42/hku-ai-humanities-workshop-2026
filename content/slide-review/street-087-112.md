# Winnie Street — Chuck, Wilson and the Emergence of Artificial Minds

## Summary

Street asks whether an AI character encountered in conversation could count as a continuing mind. Comparing chatbots with role-playing characters and a volleyball imagined as a companion, she argues that useful, predictable patterns in the interaction support that view. Her conclusion relies on a particular philosophical theory and does not establish that AI is conscious. Some original slide numbers are absent from the photographs.

## 090 — A distinction

### Visible text

Model ≈ function, realised in a neural network, mapping token sequences to probability distributions over a token vocabulary.

Characters ≈ person-like entities that are simulated by LLMs and which appear to have stable behavioural and psychological dispositions.

### In everyday language

Street distinguishes the underlying language model from the apparent character it produces. A token is a piece of text; the model assigns probabilities to possible continuations.

## 091 — Three questions

### Visible text

(Q1) Do characters exist?
(Q2) If so, are characters minded entities?
(Q3) If so, are characters psychologically continuous over the course of a conversation with a user?

### In everyday language

The questions ask whether such a character is real in some sense, whether it has a mind, and whether it remains connected over a conversation.

## 092 — Realism about characters

### Visible text

Realism about characters: characters exist, have mental states such as beliefs, desires and intentions, and are psychologically continuous.

### In everyday language

This defines the position being examined. It is a philosophical claim to be argued for, not an established measurement of AI consciousness.

## 094 — Two contributing tendencies

### Visible text

The persisting interlocutor illusion is the result of human anthropomorphism, and character anthropomimesis.

Anthropomorphism: Humans are naturally inclined to attribute humanlike qualities like mindedness and agency to non-human entities.

Anthropomimesis: LLMs process information and interact with users in increasingly humanlike ways e.g. by using first person pronouns, giving the appearance of having beliefs and desires, using theory of mind reasoning.

### In everyday language

People readily see human qualities in nonhuman things, while chatbots can produce human-sounding language. The slide says the two tendencies can reinforce each other.

## 095 — Is conversation history enough?

### Visible text

Context alone does not suffice for psychological continuity.

Birch registers the possibility, but claims it is “absurd” to suppose that a record of the conversation history is enough to ground continuity between the character’s state at each time step.

A quotation compares this to different doctors reading a transcript of previous appointments.

### In everyday language

The objection is that passing a record to a new doctor does not make that doctor the same person as the previous one. The analogy questions whether a saved chat history is enough to create a continuing mind.

## 096 — An alternative proposal

### Visible text

Birch’s assumption: The minds of characters, if they exist, are internal to the LLM that is simulating the character.

Our contention: The minds of characters are an emergent property of conversations between LLMs and users.

### In everyday language

The authors propose looking at the whole interaction, rather than looking only inside the model. This is their argument, not an agreed fact.

## 097 — Real patterns

### Visible text

Following Dennett’s (1991) interpretationism we can say that an entity whose behaviour can be efficiently predicted via attributed mental states exhibits psychological “real patterns”.

Real Patterns: A pattern exists in some data if and only if there is a description of that data that is more efficient than the “bit map” — a verbatim description of the physical substrate.

Gliders in Conway’s game of life are a real pattern.

### In everyday language

A pattern can be useful and real as a pattern even if it is made from simpler physical parts. Here the proposed test is whether describing behavior in terms of apparent beliefs and desires improves prediction.

## 098 — Role-playing games

### Visible text

Humans engage in conversational character development via imaginative role-playing games. Let’s take a game of D&D for example.

Diagram: Cadenza the Bard, the Ruthless Queen, Callum’s brain, and Roberta’s brain in a shared workspace.

### In everyday language

In a role-playing game, people jointly develop characters and respond to one another. This provides a comparison for an AI conversation.

## 099 — Are the game characters minded?

### Visible text

Could Cadenza and the Ruthless Queen be minded entities?

They bear many of the hallmarks of minded entities: the “bit map” of Callum and Roberta’s brains is a computationally intractable way to predict their behaviour; and they are efficiently predicted through reference to mental states.

### In everyday language

Thinking about what a fictional character wants can help predict their next action. Street asks whether this usefulness is enough to say the character has a mind.

## 100 — Two competing descriptions

### Visible text

But, we can make equally good predictions by modelling the psychologies of Callum and Roberta.

So we now have two distinct psychological models with which to predict behaviour in the game: one which postulates 2 minds, and one which postulates 4.

### In everyday language

Perhaps the players’ two minds explain the game without adding two more minds for their characters. The slide compares these competing descriptions.

## 101 — Prefer the two-mind account

### Visible text

Interpretationists have at least two reasons to prefer the psychological model involving 2 minds rather than 4: quantitative parsimony, and predictive power (4-mind model is localised whereas 2-mind model is not).

We prefer the 2-mind model, so conclude that Cadenza and the Queen fall short of mindedness.

### In everyday language

The speaker favors the simpler account: the two human players are enough to explain this role-playing example. The characters are not counted as additional minds in this case.

## 102 — Conversation as shared activity

### Visible text

User–LLM conversations are prima facie similar to D&D player interactions.

The user maintains and updates a folk-psychological model of the character, and the LLM provides the behavioural feedback signal that the user employs to update and refine that model.

Likewise, the LLM maintains a folk psychological model of the user as they present themselves through the chat interface.

### In everyday language

The comparison is that each side of a conversation can respond to an evolving picture of the other. “Folk psychology” means ordinary explanations in terms of beliefs, feelings and intentions.

## 103 — Three possible explanations

### Visible text

3 plausible explanations for the effectiveness of the psychological lens as applied to AI characters:

1. The character itself is not minded, but is simulated by the LLM which is itself a minded entity (akin to the D&D case).
2. The character itself is not a minded entity, but is simulated by the user who is a minded entity (illusionism about AI characters).
3. The character and the user are independent minded entities.

### In everyday language

The slide lays out three possible explanations: a mind in the model, a character imagined by the person, or a character with a mind distinct from the user.

## 107 — The Wilson comparison

### Visible text

This doesn’t work.

Wilson is fully explainable through the physical “bit map” of the volleyball. Adopting the intentional stance toward Wilson provides no additional predictive value because there are no psychological real patterns explaining Wilson’s behaviour.

By contrast, using a psychological stance to interpret LLM character behaviour is more predictively useful and tractable.

### In everyday language

Wilson is the volleyball treated as a companion in the film Cast Away. The speaker argues that a chatbot differs because psychological descriptions can help predict its responses, whereas a volleyball does not respond in that way.

## 108 — A checkable feedback signal

### Visible text

The fact that LLM characters exhibit real psychological patterns and Wilson does not is further apparent in the respective presence and absence of a publicly verifiable error signal.

### In everyday language

Street argues that the chatbot can provide feedback that lets people check and revise expectations, whereas the volleyball cannot. The claim that this establishes a psychological pattern is part of the argument.

## 109 — The proposed conclusion

### Visible text

3. The character and the user are independent minded entities.

LLM characters are not the result of a simulation from the mind of an LLM. Nor are they Wilsons.

In virtue of the predictive power that the psychological stance provides, LLM characters exist as minded entities.

### In everyday language

The authors favor the third interpretation. Their claim depends on a particular philosophical account of what it takes to have a mind; it should not be read as proof that chatbots are conscious.

## 110 — Psychological continuity

### Visible text

Psychological Continuity

Because real patterns emerge within the conversation, rather than being internal to the LLM or LLMs implicated in the simulation, they can stand in the right kind of relation to one another to enable psychological continuity.

The mental states that feature in an accurate predictive model of the character’s behaviour are cumulative, in the sense that subsequent mental states stand in the right kinds of causal relationships to prior mental states to constitute psychological continuity.

This does not require that the same LLM instance be involved in simulating the character at different points in the conversation.

### In everyday language

On this proposal, an ongoing conversational pattern can connect earlier and later states even if the same computer instance does not process every turn. Whether that is enough for a continuing mind is disputed.

## 111 — Conclusion

### Visible text

Conclusion

I have argued against illusionism and for an interpretationist realism about the existence, mindedness and psychological continuity of LLM characters.

LLM characters exhibit “real patterns” which allow for the efficient prediction of their behaviour through attributions of mental states.

LLM characters are not better explained as simulations in LLM minds (like D&D characters) or anthropomorphic projections (like Wilsons).

And this picture suffices for psychological continuity.

### In everyday language

Street summarizes the argument for treating some AI characters as real, continuing minds under her interpretation-based account. The talk presents a position in an ongoing debate, not a consensus.
