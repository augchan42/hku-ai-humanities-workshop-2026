Understanding in Large Language Models
Paul Boghossian
NYU
The Question
(Question) Are LLMs genuinely intelligent, or are they just super-sophisticated,
super-fast pattern-matchers that simulate intelligent behavior without being

intelligent?
Do LLMs solve problems in ways that are similar to the way humans do so:

i. Understand the problem being posed.
ii. Desire to answer it and intend to answer it truthfully.
iii. Reason about what it would take to answer it correctly.
iv. Know whether the resources for answering it are already available to them
or whether new evidence is required.
v. Act on those beliefs, desires and intentions to develop an answer.
vi. Communicate that answer by telling the interlocutor what the answer is.

(Q) Do LLMs solve problems the way humans do, or are they just super-
sophisticated, super-fast pattern-matchers that simulate human behavior?

Our Question, Conceptual Engineering and Apt Methodology
Perhaps what you say is true about understanding, say, in its full human version. But the crucial
question that needs answering is not Q but rather Q*:

(Q*) Do AIs instantiate the bare essence of these typical human capacities? It
might be interesting to ask whether LLMs are able to instantiate the full human
version of, say, understanding, but it’s even more important to ask whether they
are capable of understanding per se, even if it’s not in its full human version — the
bare essence of understanding, as we may put it.

(Q**) Do they instantiate concepts of understanding that are specially engineered to
suit the new kind of intelligence they represent?

However, I would insist that the most responsible and fruitful way to approach these issues is to
start with asking about the full human case, that is, to start with Q itself.

The Main Arguments for the Opposing Views
Our Question has quickly become very polarizing.

Believers are convinced that LLMs understand, know, believe, desire, intend and reason (Hinton,
Cappelen, Sutskever, Lederman). Skeptics (Marcus, Bender, Chomsky, Pearl, Pinker, Penrose)
who are just as convinced that LLMs are simply very sophisticated, very fast pattern-matchers.

In favor of the Believer’s thesis that LLMs have human-like mental capacities there is first and
foremost the fact that they are so impressive at what they can do. Best explanation is mentalistic.

What speaks in favor of the Skeptic’s position? Some Skeptics argue as follows:

i. LLMs are at bottom stochastic next-token predictors.
ii. Such processes look nothing like belief, desire or intention.

(Meaning and Understanding, M&U): LLMs understand what they are being asked and

Why would M&U be hard to vindicate? The Grounding Problem: LLMs don’t have sense organs
through which they interact with their environment (Harnad, 1990).

Meaning in LLMs
There is a view about intentionality which would not only easily get around the Grounding
Problem but also swiftly decide the question in favor of the Believers: Interpretivism.

According to Interpretivism (see Dennett, 1987), a system has such intentional states as beliefs
and desires provided it is easily interpretable as having those intentional states. And a system is
easily so interpretable provided that thinking of it as having such beliefs and desires helps explain
and predict its behavior in a certain range of cases.

The trouble is that Interpretivism is implausible. Many things are readily interpretable as having
beliefs and desires, but which it would be absurd to say really have them.

A deeper problem is that ‘interpretation’ is itself an intentional state, so it’s hard to see how
something’s being an intentional state could be explained via its being so interpretable.

Derivative vs Original Meaning.

Since the 1970s, philosophers have been interested in the ways in which our words can
sometimes mean things not because we put that meaning into them, but because we stand in
certain relations to those who did.

Understanding and Grasping Rules

Can we say that the LLMs understand what they are talking about? Like most philosophical
words, the word ‘understanding’ has been used in different ways. In particular, it is natural to
think that understanding comes in degrees.

In the weakest sense, you understand a word W when you can use W with its meaning M: it
expresses M in your mouth.

In a medium sense, you understand W when you can not only use it to mean M but you also
‘know what W means, know that it means M.’ -- I’ve put this phrase in quotation marks because
it’s not clear what it itself means and I will come back to that.

In the strongest possible sense, you understand W in the way an expert does. For example, a high
school student might understand addition in the medium sense, but not understand it as well as a
number theorist might, who would know that it is a commutative, associative, cancellative
function.

The sense in which I will be interested in LLM understanding is whether they understand in the
medium sense, which we will need to characterize.

We're interested in the minimal notion that will let the Believer’s story go through. But what
notion of understanding is this? What is it for X to know what ‘plus’ means, or what it applies
to?

Cappelen & Dever think that whatever it is, and we don’t need a view about that just now, we
can just see that LLMs understand in at least this sense. Can we just see this?

Even without a deep characterization of understanding, I think it’s clear that understanding is
not a perceptual matter in the way that C&D claim. In fact, given the way in which C&D defend
the claim, I’m not sure they believe that it is a purely perceptual matter.

Let’s distinguish sharply between two phenomena that can be easily conflated. The first is that

some fact is established purely observationally. For example, I can know, on the basis of vision
alone that Herman is wearing a (pink) shirt. On the other hand, the fact that the shirt is made in
China I don’t know purely perceptually, but I can often infer it from the fact that most shirts are
made in China (let’s say).

Now, sometimes these inferences are so obvious that they are made automatically. In such
cases, it is easy to think that the knowledge is purely perceptual, even when it is inferential.

C&D give the following eg by way of supporting the perceptual thesis. Suppose you're trying to
establish whether someone understands Mandarin. The best and easiest think you can do is get
someone who understands Mandarin to speak to them for a bit. The Mandarin speaker would
be able to establish quickly, without examining the interlocutor’s innards, whether he
understands Mandarin. So, whether someone understands Mandarin can be ascertained
observationally.

I think this reasoning conflates the two phenomena distinguished above. If the speaker were
really establishing the claim of understanding purely observationally, like in the case of color,
they would need just one utterance from the interlocutor to establish whether understanding was
present. But that’s not what we do. We need to observe their speech for a bit.

That is not perception; that is inference to the best explanation. On the assumption that you are not
aware of things that can converse fluently in a given language without understanding that
language, you are very strongly entitled to conclude that the person understands Mandarin if they
speak it fluently. However, if you become aware that the government has invested heavily in
designing super-fast humanoid robots that can pass the Turing test in Mandarin, but which are
equipped only with massive look up tables so as simulate conversing with understanding, you
might well hesitate to make that inference.

Is the best explanation for what the conversational abilities of LLMS that we're seeing that they
understand in the way humans so, or is it the alternative explanation via pattern matching?

I will try to make the case that the pattern matching explanation is the better explanation.

To evaluate the two hypotheses requires getting a little clearer about what they respectively say.
What notion of medium understanding is at issue?

One of them we have already stated. To understand something is to have a capacity that’s the
source of one’s competence with a word. What kind of thing could this source be?

You might think it could be: Being able to state correctly what the word applies to. Not that.

The most natural alternative is that it consists in some kind of ability, or disposition, to apply the
word correctly. This is closer, but what does this in turn mean?

One thing it can’t mean is that understanding in this sense consists in knowing how to apply the
word correctly in all possible circumstances.

How should we think of it then? Should we perhaps say that it consists in a high degree of
reliability in correct applications, setting the threshold at some appropriate number? 70%?

But that, too, would be absurd. Most of us learned the concept of addition at an early age. We
are full masters of what it is to add two numbers together to get their sum. But can we be said
to be reliable in 70% of all possible addition problems? Of course not.

As Kripke observed, the intuitive notion of understanding a word seems to demand construal as
(something that either is or grounds) grasp of a rule for the correct application of the word. When
you first come to understand, say, addition, this grasp of the rule for addition snaps into place.

What is crucial to recognize is that something like ‘flash grasp’ construal of understanding, in the
full human case, is crucial to a number of important features that we take our speaking and
thinking to have, features without which we would have to radically revise what we take
ourselves to be doing.

First, words you understand have infinitary satisfaction conditions. For example, your word ‘plus’
applies in just this way and not that way to any two numbers, no matter how large. They are the
satisfaction conditions for your word ‘plus.’ A word with a slightly different satisfaction
conditions would be a different concept. Quus. There are an infinite number of such alternative
functions.

How did your word ‘plus’ come to have these satisfaction conditions? The flash grasp picture
explains it: when you understand ‘plus’ as meaning plus, you grasp some finitely stateable general
rule, which you associate with the word, which determines how the word is to be correctly applied
in an infinite number of cases. Nothing apart from a general rule could do that.

Second, when you competently use a word you understand, your use of the word is explained by
your understanding.

Third, when you apply a word you understand, your application of the word is not only
explained but justified by your understanding of its meaning. Suppose I mean green by ‘green,’
and, on seeing a green thing under good lighting conditions, I utter the sentence ‘Here is a green
thing.” When I say this, what I say is not only correct, given what I mean by ‘green,’ but is also
justified by my grasp of the meaning of ‘green.’
The important point is that in the full human case language use is a rational activity. When I use
the word ‘green’ to describe this wall, I have a reason for using that word as opposed to another, a
reason that is provided by my understanding of the word. Call this the Justificatory
Requirement.

What’s the significance of the Justificatory Requirement?

There is a way of looking at the orbits of the planets on which they are following elaborate and
infinitary orbital rules. The rules could even be said explain why they move as they do, in the
way in which on some views, the laws of nature explain why things behave as they do. But what
the planets don’t do is follow their paths because they have a reason or justification for doing so.

I assume we can all recognize that the way in which we follow the rules of our language is
different from the way in which planets follow rules. And that it is an activity driven by reasons
is the essential way in which it differs from mere mechanism. These reasons are supplied by our
understanding and are consciously accessible to us: I know why I am disposed to apply the word
‘green’ to this green wall.

This shows a connection between this notion of understanding and certain notions of
consciousness.

Of course, we already have such a connection in the case of terms like ‘green,’ whose meanings
do not seem graspable without experiential acquaintance with their referent.

But what this Kripkenstein-inspired argument shows is that even in the case of logical or
mathematical words, where no such acquaintance requirement is in place, there is still a
potentially strong connection between understanding and consciousness.

Testing for Grasp
We see whether they are able to apply the rule correctly in easy and moderately difficult cases,
while allowing for a much higher rate of errors in harder cases.

Why do we proceed in this way? The answer is obvious. Typical humans have limited working
memories and limited powers of concentration. When those limitations are irrelevant — in the
easy cases — we expect near perfection from someone who has grasped the rule.

Reply to the Problem of Easy Cases: Humans Make Simple Errors, Too

There are two possible replies to this line of argument. The first one notes that even humans are
capable of elementary errors consistent with their understanding the subject at hand, so we've set
the bar too high.

Look at the multiplication case. If a human were to make an error in such a case, it would be
due not to their not knowing what multiplication is, but to a performance error, due to memory
and attention limitations. But these limitations don’t apply to LLMs with their massive compute
power and blazing speed.

Given a choice between two different types of explanation for these errors:

■ understanding plus performance limitations versus no understanding, and
■ being misled by patterns in the training set —

it does seem as though the latter is the more plausible explanation in the LLM case, while the
former is the more plausible explanation in the human case.

Reply: These Errors Will Eventually Disappear

I am doubtful that there will come a time when even the possibility of such elementary errors will
have been decisively eliminated. Easy errors arise because they are outlier cases. I assume that,
so long as the training database is finite, there will always be outlier cases where the system can
be tempted to behave in these uncomprehending ways. That’s why I’m doubtful that the
possibility of such errors can be definitively eliminated.

LLMs and Grasping Rules
But what if the possibility of all such errors is eliminated?

The main argument for not attributing strong understanding that doesn’t depend on the
possibility of easy errors is that there seems to be nothing corresponding to the ‘snapping into
place of the grasp of a rule’ that then stands in extension-determining, explanatory and
justificatory relations to a potential infinity of subsequent uses that we saw to be characteristic of
the human case.

There are people who think that there is evidence that LLMs cotton onto rules as is shown in the
phenomenon known as ‘grokking.’ Grokking is a phenomenon in machine learning where a
neural network first appears to memorize its training data and later seems to suddenly discover a
general rule that lets it perform well on new cases.

There are two questions here:

(1) Does the network come to represent something general, rather than merely memorize
instances?

(2) And does that amount to grasping a rule in the sense in which a human grasps one?
On the first question, there is quite strong evidence that, at least in some cases, the answer is yes.

But can this really be said to be an infinitary rule with an infinite domain? Ordinary addition is a
function on the natural numbers, so there are infinitely many possible cases.

Most classic grokking experiments use finite domains such as addition modulo 97. Perfect
generalization there does not by itself demonstrate mastery of an infinite extension. A skeptic
can say that the network has discovered an efficient compression of a finite function rather than
grasped a rule governing infinitely many cases. :

What could possibly determine about a particular LLM node that it instantiates the plus rule and
not something that diverges from it at very large numbers? The usual ways of pinning down
interpretability in non-humans — the designer's intentions, the telos of the thing (assuming it was
a biological organism) — do not apply here.

Also in doubt would be the usual counterfactuals — if I were to extend the thing’s lifetime, it
would do such-and-so; unlike ordinary devices, like calculators, or symbolic computers, we are
much less clear about these counterfactuals. The plus/quus problem is a real one for these

networks.

Even putting this issue about finitude to one side, we would still be missing the Justificatory
requirement. For there seems to be nothing in an LLM that amounts to acting on a reason, to
the machine’s having a justification for giving one answer as opposed to another.

Yet justification is what turns it from mere mechanical action to something that’s rationally
assessable. Rational assessability requires doing something for a reason, and not just
mechanically. And that in turn seems to involve treating something as a reason and doing
something because of that. (This structure is also present in reasoning itself.)

Is there a More Deflationary Understanding of Understanding That’s Still Adequate?

The question is: What notion of understanding would be left if we gave up both on infinitary
extensions and on language use being a rational activity? These are the questions that need to be
thought through.
